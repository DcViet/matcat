Here is an example I made up. You ask a carpenter to build you a chair. He does not ask whether you prefer an old style or a new one. He asks: is this chair for eating or for reading, for sitting a long time or a short one? Once you answer, the chair already has half its shape. How high the legs are, how far the back leans, you will find they come out of your answer.

Le Corbusier wanted architects to treat the house in exactly this way. His sentence is more famous than his idea: "a house is a machine for living in," from *Vers une architecture* (1923), published in English as *Towards a New Architecture*, on page 95 according to my notes. On its own it sounds cold. Read the whole passage and it does not.

## What That Sentence Packs In

According to my notes, he uses the word "machine" to say that a house should be thought of as a tool: for bathing, for taking in sun, for hot and cold water, for heating as you wish, for keeping food, for hygiene. By the same logic, a chair is a "machine for sitting." In the last chapter he rewrites it as "house-machine" and "house-tool": drop the dead concepts, look at the problem critically, and we get a house that is healthy and as beautiful as the tools we use every day. The order is clear: reason must be satisfied first, and then emotion arrives.

The English translator, Frederick Etchells, even opens with a note asking British and American readers not to panic at the phrase. It is not a call to give up beauty.

## The Airplane Teaches How to State a Problem

In the chapter on airplanes he tells of two approaches. Ader wanted to fly like a bird, so he built the "Bat," and it never left the ground. Wright and Farman posed a different problem: lifting a solid body in the air. The result was harsh to look at, but it flew. The lesson, in his view, is not in the shape of the plane. It lies in the fact that when the problem is stated correctly, the solution finds itself.

Then he says the problem of the house has not yet been stated. He tries. A house is a shelter against heat, cold, rain, thieves, and the curious; it is a container for light and sun. Inside there are cells for cooking, working, and private life. The minimum number of rooms he lists is four: one for cooking and eating, one for work, one for washing, one for sleeping.

Stating a problem through the things people do is close to what I wrote in [Verbs Open Up Possibilities](/en/bai-viet/dong-tu-mo-ra-kha-nang/), even though the two start from different places.

## A Manual for the Person Who Lives There

From that problem he wrote a list of requirements that every family should demand of a landlord, called the "Manual of the Dwelling" (pp. 115 to 123). He adds that it should be handed out to mothers. A few of its items:

- A bathroom facing south, among the larger rooms, with a glass wall opening to a balcony for sun.
- A dressing room next door, so no one dresses in the bedroom.
- One very large living room instead of several small ones.
- Plain walls in the bedroom, living, and dining rooms.
- Built-in furniture in place of most loose pieces, closets set into the walls.
- Hidden or diffused lighting, windows with ventilation openings, a bare floor with few rugs.

Reading the list, you can see right away what he dislikes: chandeliers, curtains, the sideboard, flowered wallpaper that paintings cannot hang against. My notes also record that the setting is France in 1923 and that his tone carries a class color, for example his advice not to shut servants up under the roof. Some items are better read as period material than as advice for your own home.

## The Car and the Parthenon: One Standard

The second lesson is about the standard. He looks at the automobile and the Parthenon as results of selection: a standard formed through trial and competition, which later hands keep refining. For the Parthenon, my notes say it came out of selection on a standard of Greek temples that had been developing for a century.

From there he moves to the mass-production house, in a chapter near the end of the book (pp. 225 to 266). He speaks of a need for 500,000 houses under the Loucheur program in France. He suggests putting up the concrete frame first, then fitting doors, windows, and cupboards, all made to one shared unit of measure, into the empty shell, and filling in the walls last. Working in the reverse of the usual order, he says, saves many months. One of his models is called Citrohan, a pun on the car maker Citroën, because he wants us to think of the house the way people think of a car chassis. That is also why I chose a Citroën for the cover.

## What the Book Does Not Say, and What People Say Against It

Several things people attach to this book are not in it. According to my library notes, it does not contain the "five points of a new architecture," the Modulor, or Villa Savoye; those belong to a later period. This is a manifesto, in a clipped voice that shouts slogans.

On criticism, I will record only the two lines found in my notes. Venturi, Scott Brown, and Izenour argue that the very models of the ship and the grain silo became icons of Modernism even as its people said they were only learning geometry; they also see Le Corbusier's buildings as looking more like ocean liners than like the Parthenon. The duck and the decorated shed in [The Duck and the Shed with a Sign](/en/bai-viet/con-vit-va-nha-che-co-bien-hieu/) are a continuation of that argument. The second line is Banham's: he holds that Le Corbusier's knowledge of the environment did not all make it into his buildings, for instance glass walls turned south that overheated, after which he had to add opening windows and invent the brise-soleil. I told the story of two ways to keep a house warm or cool in [Build a Tent or Light a Fire](/en/bai-viet/dung-leu-hay-dot-lua/).

The notes on the book also name a class bias: he treats ornament as the business of "peasants" and proportion as the business of cultured people.

## Try It Yourself

Pick a room you use often, a bedroom or a corner where you work. Write down five things you do there every day, as verbs: sleep, read, store clothes, work, make phone calls. Then look around and ask of each object: which of these does it serve? An object that cannot answer is not necessarily one to throw out, since we keep some things for looks or for memory. But you will see which part of the room is a tool and which part you chose to add yourself.
