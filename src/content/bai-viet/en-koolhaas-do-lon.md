One Saturday afternoon you ride down into the basement of a big shopping mall. You park, take the escalator up to the supermarket, stop at the bookstore, have dinner on the fourth floor, and then catch a movie on the fifth. Three hours later you are back in the basement, and only as you come up the ramp do you find out that it rained hard while you were inside.

I made this scene up as an example; it is not a memory. But you have probably stood across the street from a building like that and looked at it: a few blank walls, a few signs. Where is the movie theater, where is the supermarket? From outside there is no way to tell. The architect Rem Koolhaas has a word for buildings of this kind: *Bigness*.

## Where the Word Comes From

Koolhaas was born in 1944 in Rotterdam and worked as a journalist before he turned to architecture. He wrote *Delirious New York* (1978), then *S,M,L,XL* (Monacelli Press, 1995), and received the Pritzker Prize in 2000. His office, OMA, does the building, while its sister branch AMO does the research. The word Bigness comes from the second book.

I should say up front how this piece reads the idea: through a set of 13 slides made as coursework by two students, Mark Alves and Harriet Webb, around 2011, which quote a few lines from the book. So it retells Bigness in their words, and wherever I add my own thinking, I will say so.

## Too Big to Be a Piece of the Street

According to the slides, Bigness is the condition of a building so large that it no longer belongs to any pattern of streets. At best it puts up with what is around it; otherwise it ignores it altogether.

Here is how I understand that sentence, and this reading is my own. An ordinary shophouse is a piece of its row: it stands about as tall as its neighbors, its door opens onto the sidewalk, and the people inside are one step away from the street. Put many of them side by side and you get a street. A building that takes up a whole block is a different thing: it has its own parking levels, its own walkways, even its own "plaza" tucked inside. The kind of life that, in [People Go Where People Are](/en/bai-viet/nguoi-den-noi-co-nguoi/), I described as happening on the sidewalk has now moved indoors. The street outside is left as a road for cars to arrive by.

## The Front Stops Telling What Is Inside

The second point the slides record is that the facade separates from what we know about the interior. With a small house, you can guess a good deal from outside: this window is probably a bedroom, that large pane is the living room. I am used to thinking that the outside of a house should tell the truth about its inside.

With a very big building, that habit stops working. There is too much inside, and it sits too deep behind the skin, like the movie theater and the parking levels in my opening example. The skin cannot tell all of it, so it becomes a job of its own.

This reminds me of the decorated shed I wrote about in [The Duck and the Shed with a Sign](/en/bai-viet/con-vit-va-nha-che-co-bien-hieu/): the building takes care of what goes on inside, and the symbol is attached separately on the outside. I think Koolhaas belongs next to Venturi: both are willing to look straight at the kind of city that usually gets dismissed as ordinary, though each arrives at a different answer.

| | Ordinary-size house | Very big building |
|---|---|---|
| The street | It is a piece of the street | It stands apart; at best it puts up with its surroundings |
| The front | Lets you guess the inside | Separate from the inside |
| Shared life | On the sidewalk, at the door | Moved inside the building |

## A Condition Almost Nobody Has Thought Through

The slides copy down one more idea: Bigness is a condition with "almost no thinker." As I understand it, people keep building bigger because money and technology now allow it, while very few sit down to work out what rules building that big might need.

Bigness has a close relative, the *Generic City*: a city that sprawls, looks the same everywhere, has no history, and repeats one simple module over and over. According to the slides, Koolhaas treats the skyscraper as the final building type of that city, because it makes no difference whether you set it in a rice field or downtown. Being generic also means ignoring context, but it does not have to be big.

## What Koolhaas Does with It

By this point it would be easy to assume Koolhaas is complaining. The two slide authors read him differently. In their account, he thinks Bigness is less of a problem when the people making it take the place seriously. He starts from the mess of the existing situation, including the culture and the country, draws diagrams to peel it apart layer by layer, and then sets up a program that weaves many uses together. He sees the crowding that comes with a big building, and he values it.

Antje Steinmüller's lecture series (CCA, 2010–2011) has an example of that kind of diagram. At the Seattle Library, OMA grouped the program into blocks stacked on top of one another: reading, meeting, staff, parking, children. The building in the cover photo, the CCTV headquarters in Beijing, is also his work.

He does not throw out everything old, either. The slides quote his point that some "architectural devices" stay almost unchanged over time; they are limited, yet they always work. His example is the staircase.

## What I Still Doubt

My first doubt is about the source. Student slides are short on words and loosely written, and in one place they contradict themselves: they say Koolhaas does not rely on precedent, and then say he keeps the staircase. To use this idea seriously you would want to read the essay "Bigness, or the problem of Large" in *S,M,L,XL* itself.

My second doubt is about the threshold. How big does a building have to be before it counts as Bigness? The slides give no number. The two students seem unsure as well: they ask whether a huge kit home in the suburbs is a case of Bigness, and leave the question open.

The third doubt is my own. The phrase "at best it puts up with its context" can easily turn into an excuse for the makers of big buildings to stop thinking about the neighbors. However big a building gets, its base still meets the sidewalk, and people on foot still have to walk around it every day.

## Try It on a Big Building Near You

The next time you pass a shopping mall, a large hospital, or a train station, stand across the street for a few minutes. Guess what is behind that wall, then go in and see how much you got right. Count the doors that open onto the sidewalk along the base of the building, and compare with the row of shophouses across the street. Then ask yourself: is this building putting up with the street, ignoring it, or has it found a kinder way to live with it?
