This example is mine, not a memory. It is a Mekong Delta afternoon in August, dark clouds arrive fast, and you are sitting on a wooden bench out on the porch. Rain pours down and you stay dry, because the roof reaches out to cover you. Water runs off into the yard, then the garden, then the canal in front of the house. The breeze still slips through the louvered doors. The whole house is doing one job: letting water, sun and wind pass through without making the people inside suffer for it.

The old houses that survive in the south suggest many people thought this way long ago. This piece looks at three specific houses, then ties them to a bigger idea: reading the land of the south through two words, "mountain" and "water."

## Three Houses, Three Ages

Most of the old houses that remain in the south were built in the late 19th and early 20th centuries. I picked three to look at closely.

| House | Where | Year | Worth a look |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ba Đức house | Cái Bè, Tiền Giang | Built 1850, restored 1938 | Right by the river; front hall for worship and guests, rear hall for living, with a small open court between |
| Bình Thủy house (the Dương family ancestral house) | Cần Thơ | Built 1870, renovated early 20th century | Raised floor, 24 ironwood columns, a central yard paved with Chinese brick |
| Huỳnh Thủy Lê house | Sa Đéc, Đồng Tháp | Built 1895, rebuilt 1917 | Thick brick walls around a timber frame, yin-yang tile roof, gable ends curved upward |

I arranged this table myself. The first two were originally built of wood under tile roofs. The third, after its rebuilding, looks like a French villa from outside, while the interior is furnished in the Vietnamese and Chinese manner. In the cover photo you can see that mix at Bình Thủy: arched doors, balustrades and plasterwork in a Western vein, but a low tiled roof and a row of louvered shutters that look entirely local.

## Beside the Water

The Ba Đức house sits close to the riverbank, on a small lane about three kilometers from the center of Cái Bè district. Its floor stands roughly 10 cm above the ground to keep flood water out and cut down on insects. Before laying the tiles, the builders spread a layer of salt underneath. Bình Thủy has the same layer of salt, about 10 cm thick according to one news article, while its floor is raised about a meter.

Those two numbers differ a lot, so I will not conclude "the floor must be this high." What I can take from them is the way of thinking: water has to be planned for first, from where the house sits, to how high the floor stands, to what goes under the paving. One study of Southern old houses also notes that houses usually face east, southeast or south, but a house on a river or beside a road no longer picks a direction. Here is how I read that detail: for people in the delta, the canal is both road and water supply, so at times it comes before the compass.

## Living Under the Roof

The frame is timber. At Bình Thủy, according to Vietnam News, there are six rows with 24 ironwood columns in total, each about 30 cm across, tied together with carved beams. The house is about 22 m long and 16 m deep, on a plot of roughly 6,000 square meters. It has many doors and windows, and the article stresses that the house has no fans or air conditioning.

The roof is what keeps off sun and rain. The Huỳnh Thủy Lê house has yin-yang tiles, with both gable ends curved up like the prow of a boat. Its brick walls are about 30 to 40 cm thick, wrapped around the timber frame. The Ba Đức house is also roofed in yin-yang or fish-scale tiles, with rafters and purlins all in wood.

These descriptions come with no temperature or wind measurements, so I will not claim the house is cooler by any number of degrees. All I can say is how it was built: a thick roof against the sun, many openings for air, a floor lifted away from damp, materials from the region. Reyner Banham split the job of making a place livable into two routes, building a tent or lighting a fire, which I covered in [Build a Tent or Light a Fire](/en/bai-viet/dung-leu-hay-dot-lua/). I think the old southern house takes the first route: let the shell do all it can before thinking about machines. In [Geoffrey Bawa: A Tropical House Is a Place to Pass Through and Stay](/en/cong-trinh/bawa-nha-nhiet-doi-di-xuyen-qua/) you will meet that same porch and roof again, in another tropical country.

## Garden and Yard Are Part of the House

The Ba Đức house stands on a plot of two mẫu (about 20,000 square meters), with a wide front yard planted with grass, trees and flowers. Bình Thủy has a front garden, a back garden, and a central yard paved with Chinese brick and set with potted plants. At Ba Đức, the open court between front hall and rear hall brings a patch of sky into the middle of the house.

Seen this way, "the house" does not stop at the wall. It is the timber block under its roof plus the empty spaces around it: yard, garden, court, landing at the water. ## Mountain and Water: One House, One Delta

In the conference slide deck "From the thatched roof to the Urban Galaxy of the South" by architect Lý Thái Sơn (128 pages), the section on urban mountain-and-water (pp. 55–66) sets "mountain" beside mass, buildings, the solid, and "water" beside voids, water surfaces, landscape, the empty. He compares this way of seeing with Western figure-ground. In the section on the south (pp. 83–100), he reads the whole delta as a mountain-and-water cross-section: the east higher, on old alluvium, with the Đồng Nai river system; the west lower, on new alluvium, with the Tiền and Hậu rivers.

The deck's readings by cosmic cycles are cultural belief, and I do not use them as grounds for anything. But the pair "solid and void" does work, and it fits the houses above. At small scale, the house block is the mountain, while yard, garden and canal are the water. At large scale, cities are solid blocks set among rivers and fields, and the deck calls the chain of linked cities an "urban galaxy" (megalopolis). That reading is mine: the old house is a small galaxy, and the delta is a big house, where every solid and every void has a reason.

## The Old Houses Are Vanishing

One survey in Đồng Nai found that most of the old houses there have been torn down or rebuilt. The ones still standing, like the three above, are usually listed or have someone looking after them. Losing an old house means losing a way of dealing with water and sun that we have not yet measured or learned from.

## Try It on a House Near You

Next time you pass an old house, in the south or anywhere else, ask three questions. Where does the rainwater go? Where does the afternoon sun stop: roof, porch or leaves? Which part of the plot is "mountain" and which is "water"? Then tell me what you found.
