This example is my own invention. You stand on the sidewalk of a craft street, in front of a shop about as wide as your outstretched arms. You expect a small room selling things. Then the owner points and says, "Keep going straight." You pass the counter, step out into a small yard open to the sky, cross another room, cross another yard, and by the time you reach the kitchen you realize you have walked a long way into the middle of a city block. Hanoi's Old Quarter is full of houses like this. People call them tube houses.

I wanted to answer a question that sounds naive: if a house is walled in on every side and only cracks open toward the street, where does its light and air come from?

## One side to the street, three sides of wall

In *Tropical Sustainable Architecture* (edited by Bay Joo Hwa and Ong Boon Lay, 2006), chapter 12 is by Shoichi Ota and describes the "36 streets" district. According to him, the tube houses there are 2.5 to 5.5 m wide, sometimes more than 60 m deep, and packed side by side to fill the whole block. Each house opens only to the street; the other three sides are shared walls. Neighbors cannot pass through to each other, so to meet, they go out to the street (pp. 221-227).

The Old Quarter covers roughly 100 hectares and has 76 streets, and it began forming in the 11th century. The number 36 is a traditional name that does not match the real count of streets. Each street was once a guild street with a communal hall for worship and meetings. Density, in Ota's account, is about 1,000 people per hectare, similar to a housing estate on the edge of Tokyo.

The frontage matters most: it is both the shop and the link between the house and the market. The side and rear walls are just boundaries. So the house stays narrow and makes up for it in depth.

## Walking in from the street

One house in the district has been restored for visitors: 87 Ma May Street. Press reports give it a frontage of about 5 m, a length of over 28 m and an area near 157.6 m2. I use these only as rough figures, because I have not seen an official survey, and the reports disagree on when it was built, some saying early in the 19th century and others late. It was recognized as a national heritage site in 2004.

The order of rooms, though, is described much the same way across reports. The shop comes first. Behind it is a narrow yard with no roof, called a skylight well, which lets in daylight and catches rainwater for household use. Next is the middle house, then another yard, and at the far end the kitchen. Upstairs, a wooden staircase leads to rooms for the ancestral altar, for receiving guests and for sleeping, one after another from front to back, opening to the yards through large wooden doors. The columns, rafters and stairs are all ironwood.

Seen this way, the plan is a chain of solid and void: house, yard, house, yard, house. Each void is a window to the sky for the two rooms beside it. That is why the middle room is not shut in darkness, and why the kitchen at the very back can still see the sky.

## Light and air come down from above

A courtyard does two jobs. The first is easy to see: daylight drops in from above, so the deep rooms are bright by day. The second is harder to see: air. The air above a yard warms up, gets lighter and rises, and cooler air from elsewhere flows in to replace it. I explained this more fully in the piece on [the Jalan Elok terrace house](/en/bai-viet/lat-nguoc-nha-lien-ke-jalan-elok/), a narrow, deep house in Singapore. I have no measurements to say how strong the breeze in a real tube house is. What I can say with confidence is the direction: three solid walls block the wind, and a courtyard leaves the air an exit.

There is another benefit. Light that enters from high up through a narrow opening is usually softer than light hitting a street front head-on. I wrote about that in [Good Light Is Not Plenty of Light](/en/bai-viet/anh-sang-tot-khong-phai-anh-sang-nhieu/).

## When the yards get built over

Ota also records what happened later. After the revolution, many houses were divided among several families, and the people at the back had to walk through a side corridor. The houses needed more living space, so the yards were gradually built over. The ventilation and daylight that had been the tube house's strength disappeared, and people used more electricity. In summer the grid was overloaded and sometimes cut.

That is why he argues for "dynamic conservation": keep the character of the space (house joined to yard, several households living around a yard) rather than only the facade. The book is also clear that the chapter was written by a Japanese researcher for an audience of engineers, so the real lives of residents get little attention.

## Try it yourself

Next time you walk past a row of town houses, stop and picture what lies behind that narrow front. If you ever get inside a house that deep, count how many ways light reaches the farthest room: a window, a skylight, or only a lamp left on. And if you live in a narrow house yourself, find the spot where you can still see the sky, then ask yourself: if we closed that spot off, what would the whole house lose?
