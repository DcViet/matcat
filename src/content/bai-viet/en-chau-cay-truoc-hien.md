In mid-November, the headline of a news story about a townhouse project in the Ciputra urban area of Hanoi spoke of bringing greenery into every floor. This essay does not discuss that building, because I have only read the headline. I want to ask an older question instead: where does a plant actually do its job?

## A narrow forecourt by the door

Imagine a unit on the sixteenth floor in Singapore. The door opens onto a public corridor, and just outside it is a narrow forecourt, half inside and half outside, big enough for a few pots. This scene is rebuilt from the book's figures. It is not the story of one real person.

The book *Tropical Sustainable Architecture* (2006), edited by Bay Joo Hwa and Ong Boon Lay, has a chapter on Bedok Court. The estate has 280 units in three blocks from 4 to 20 storeys, designed by the architect Cheng Jian Fenn between 1982 and 1985. Its key feature is that each unit has a semi-open forecourt that connects to a corridor which works like a street in the air. The idea draws on the forecourt of the kampong house, the village dwelling of Southeast Asia, where people sit outside and talk with their neighbors.

## Deep enough to sit in

A pleasant forecourt is not enough. It must also be deep enough. The book gives a specific threshold: a forecourt about 2 metres deep, plus a corridor 1.4 metres wide, has room for chairs and children playing, and shade over about 57% of its area. A narrower one holds only a few pots, and people do not stay there long.

The resident survey gives results worth noticing. About 86% of respondents said they liked the forecourt and used it more than once a week. About 90% did not feel a lack of privacy. The room stays private, but residents accepted a little less privacy in exchange for seeing one another every day.

Jan Gehl wrote that people go where other people are, and a forecourt by the door is one way to put those people inside the building itself. You can read more in [People Go Where People Are](/en/bai-viet/nguoi-den-noi-co-nguoi/).

## How much the plants cool

The plants do cool the space. In the same chapter, the book reports that a forecourt with many plants was about 0.4 to 2.5 degrees cooler than one without, depending on the floor and the season. One measurement on the sixteenth floor found that at 12:30 in July, a forecourt with dense plants was about 2.3 degrees cooler than a bare one, when shade and wind were roughly equal.

This runs against the instinct of many people who live in hot places. We tend to think wind decides how cool we feel. But the book shows that in a semi-open space, sun affects the sense of heat more strongly than wind does. So plants do two jobs: they block the sun directly and they cool the surfaces around the forecourt. Plants also add humidity, so if the forecourt is closed to the wind, the position of the plants matters.

## Plants that get neighbors talking

The book records a small detail that I find the most valuable: residents with more plants by their homes knew more of their neighbors. The reason is not hard to see. Plants need watering every morning, dead leaves need pruning, and someone asks why a branch has died. Each of these tasks brings people out to the forecourt, and being outside means meeting.

This also sustains itself. The household looks after its own plants, so no central maintenance team is needed. The book compares this with rooftop gardens or large parks, which need budgets and managers. A forecourt lives according to the needs of the household that owns it.

## Townhouses and apartments in Vietnam

This section is my own thinking, not something the book says. The book is about Singapore, an equatorial climate, and an estate with controlled entry. Hanoi has a cold season, so a forecourt there would need a sliding screen or curtain that closes in winter. Many apartment buildings in Vietnam have also enclosed their balconies with iron grilles or glass, and in those cases the space for pots disappears.

The book also offers a lesson about planning. Singapore's regulations from 1986 counted total floor area, so many later projects no longer kept wide forecourts as Bedok Court did. If you are designing a townhouse or an apartment in Vietnam, I think three things are worth doing:

- Leave a loggia with an open railing, deep enough for two or three pots and a chair.
- Check the current building code to see whether a forecourt counts toward floor area, because that is often where forecourts get cut first.
- On a west-facing side, add louvres or a tree to block the afternoon sun, which is the hardest heat to live with.

The Hanoi tube house also has light wells and courtyards that bring wind and light deep into the house, as described in [The Tube House of Hanoi's Old Quarter: Narrow Across, Deep Within](/en/bai-viet/nha-ong-pho-co-ha-noi-hep-ma-sau/). The front forecourt is the part of that story that connects the house to the street.

Next time you walk past a row of townhouses, look for a plant pot outside a door. If you see one, guess who waters it every morning. That person may be the neighbor you will get to know first.
