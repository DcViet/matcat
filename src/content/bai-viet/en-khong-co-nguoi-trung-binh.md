I made this example up, but you have probably met it somewhere. A newly built kitchen, the wall cabinets hung in a neat row, the paint still fresh. The homeowner is a woman about a meter and a half tall. She can't reach the top shelf, so the spice jars all end up down on the counter and the top shelf stays empty year-round. The carpenter was not careless. He hung the cabinets at a number his hands knew well, the number that "suits a normal person."

So who is that normal person?

## The Average Person Does Not Exist

Julius Panero and Martin Zelnik wrote *Human Dimension & Interior Space* for people who design interiors, and in chapter 2 they put it in one short sentence: "There simply is no 'average man.'"

The first reason sits inside the word average. If you take the number in the exact middle of a group and make it your design dimension, then by definition half the group loses out. A shelf set at the middle reach is out of reach for the half with shorter arms.

The second reason is harder to see. A person whose height falls in the middle of the group does not necessarily have arms, legs, or shoulders that fall in the middle too. The book cites figures from Hertzberg: people who are average in two measurements at once make up only about 7% of the population, in three measurements about 3%, in four measurements under 2%. By ten measurements, no one is left. Designing for "the average person" turns out to mean designing for someone nobody has ever met.

## What a Percentile Is

Anthropometry is the measuring of the human body. To make those measurements usable, people rank them from small to large and divide the range into a hundred steps, called percentiles. The 5th percentile of reach is the value that 5% of the group match or fall short of. The 95th percentile is the value that 95% of the group match or fall below. The 50th percentile sits close to the middle.

Since nobody can make one object that fits everyone, designers usually drop the two far ends and aim at the middle 90%.

Two things are easy to get wrong. A percentile belongs to one measurement, not to one person: someone whose height is at the 50th percentile may have leg length at the 40th and reach at the 55th. And "the 95th percentile person" is just an imaginary dummy, the same as the average person. The book has a figure of three real people, and the line joining each one's percentiles zigzags up and down from one measurement to the next.

## Size Reach for Short People, Clearance for Tall People

![Diagram of an axis of measurements from small to large: reach is chosen around the 5th percentile, clearance around the 95th, and adjustable things cover at least the middle 90%](/images/khong-co-nguoi-trung-binh-en.svg)

So which step do you pick? The two authors say to look at what people are about to do with the thing.

| What the user does | Size it for | Examples given in the book |
|---|---|---|
| Reach | Small people, around the 5th percentile | Shelves, buttons, handle heights |
| Pass through | Large people, around the 95th percentile | Corridors, the space under a table, low ceilings |
| Adjust to fit | A range that covers at least 90% of users | Swivel chairs, movable shelves |

Their reasoning is plain. If the person with the shortest arms can reach it, people with longer arms can too, and the reverse does not hold. Wherever the largest person fits through, smaller people fit through as well.

I like this way of sorting because it starts from verbs. "Reach" and "pass through" are two different actions, so the same group of people has to be read from two different ends of the table. I wrote about starting a design from verbs in [Verbs Open Up Possibilities](/en/bai-viet/dong-tu-mo-ra-kha-nang/).

The book adds a few more cautions. Some cases are in between, such as armrest height, and for those you take a value near the middle. The same measurement can flip depending on what you want: a partition meant for privacy is sized by the eye level of tall people, while one that everybody should be able to see over is sized by the eye level of short people. And don't be mechanical about 5 and 95: if lowering a shelf by a few centimeters covers 98 to 99% of users and costs next to nothing, lower it.

Now back to that kitchen. According to the book's section on kitchens, the highest shelf should be set by the standing reach of a 5th percentile woman. Wall cabinets always end up with high shelves that small people can't reach and low ones that make you stoop, so the book treats an adjustable cabinet system as the sensible answer.

## Whose Measurements?

Before you open a table, you have to know which group you are designing for: age, sex, occupation, ethnicity. Most of the original data in the book was taken from American military men aged 18 to 45, a group that looks little like an architect's clients. The book includes a chart of heights from many countries; the tallest and shortest samples differ by nearly 20 cm, and the Vietnamese sample is among the shortest. So I don't copy any of the book's numbers into a Vietnamese home. I borrow the way of thinking.

Bodies are also measured unclothed and barefoot, so in use you have to add clothing and shoes. And if the piece is for a single person, the book advises measuring that person directly, the way a tailor does.

## A Table Can't Replace the Real Chair

The two authors hedge their own book. Section 2.7 notes that the data is incomplete, especially for children, older people, people with disabilities, and for measurements of the body in motion. A table of numbers can't stand in for common sense, function, and the designer's eye.

The drawings in the third part of the book, in the authors' words, are only "reference models" for first assumptions, not solutions. For seating and anywhere comfort matters, they recommend building a full-size mockup, having people sit in it, and then revising. Nutsch's book on furniture, which I also read, says the same thing: the numbers are a starting point, and you should always build a 1:1 model with the person who will use it.

I have one more reminder for people in the trade: architects easily take themselves as the model, and what fits me may not fit most people.

The cover image is Leonardo's drawing of a body fitted inside a circle and a square. Panero and Zelnik's introduction also starts from Vitruvius and Leonardo, then observes that the study of the body has long leaned toward beautiful proportion more than toward measuring how people use things. They propose going the other way, "designing from the man out." Out on the street, Jan Gehl also starts by standing and watching real people, as I describe in [People Go Where People Are](/en/bai-viet/nguoi-den-noi-co-nguoi/).

Next time you plan to put up a shelf, try this before you call the carpenter: stick a strip of masking tape on the wall at the height you have in mind, then ask the shortest person in the house to reach for it. If there is a tight spot, such as the gap between the bed and the wardrobe, ask the biggest person in the house to walk through. You will know which number to change before it turns into wood.
