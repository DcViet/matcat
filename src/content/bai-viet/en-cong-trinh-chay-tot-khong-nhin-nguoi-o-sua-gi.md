There is a cheap test anyone can run. Walk into a house or a café that has been in use for a few months, and look at what the people there have changed on their own. A chair dragged out of its original spot, a sheet of paper taped over a window to block the sun, a door that is always shut even though everyone knows it was built to open. This example is mine, not a story about any particular place. But every one of those changes is a comment, and usually a more honest one than any report.

"Does this building perform well?" sounds like an engineer's question, with instruments and tables of numbers. Reading several books on the subject, I found the answer sits in two places: the numbers, and how people use the building in real life. Here are five questions I pulled out, which you can ask of your own house.

## A test that already exists: used as intended

In *Experiencing Architecture* (1959, chapter I), Rasmussen offers a very short test: a good building is one that is used exactly as its designer intended. He compares the architect to a gardener. Pick a plant the soil cannot feed and the intention, however beautiful, fails. But he also warns that a building must be designed ahead of time to survive ways of use nobody has predicted yet.

Those two ideas pull against each other, and the tension between them is where we learn the most.

## Question 1: what did the residents change after moving in?

In [Geoffrey Bawa: A Tropical House Is a Place to Pass Through and Stay](/en/cong-trinh/bawa-nha-nhiet-doi-di-xuyen-qua/) I mentioned the one high-rise he built, in Colombo. According to Tan and Robson, the building was designed in 1972, finished in 1978, and rises 12 floors. It was planned to let the wind pass through: precast vents, precast concrete slots at sill height that let air in even with the windows shut, a shallow floor plan, and deep eaves that cut low sun. But right after it was finished, the occupants, foreign specialists of the Mahaweli ministry, divided up the open plan and installed window air conditioners.

The building was never used as intended, and no one ever came back to evaluate it after people moved in. Bawa grew discouraged and left it out of his publications. It was only published in 1998 (Robson and Daswatte). By 2005, Tan and Robson recorded what the occupants had done.

![Four pairs: what Bawa intended at the Mahaweli Building and what the occupants did after moving in](/images/cong-trinh-chay-tot-khong-nhin-nguoi-o-sua-gi-en.svg)

Dust and traffic noise made people shut the windows. Papers blew around on the upper floors. The vents at sill height were boarded up with plywood, because of rain splash and because of vapor leaking when the air conditioning ran. Thick cloth curtains were drawn closed. Rooms were cut up by tall partitions into a maze that blocked both light and wind. From the occupants' side, every one of these choices makes sense. The wind is nice, but dust and noise travel with it.

The two authors propose fixes: outdoor noise barriers, sliding panels to adjust airflow (like WOHA's "monsoon windows" at One Moulmein Rise), ceiling fans, a mixed mode for the hot season, perforated blinds, shading outside the building, and an open plan with low partitions plus a few enclosed rooms near the core. The lesson they draw is that natural ventilation has to be controllable, that users need to be educated, and that post-occupancy evaluation is needed.

One limit needs stating plainly. The data in their paper come from observation and interviews, not measurement, and the building has been altered so much that it is hard to treat as evidence for either side.

## Question 2: can the building's promise be measured?

In 1907, at the Huguet silk mill (Wayland, New York), Willis Carrier for the first time guaranteed the performance of an air-conditioning system, instead of guaranteeing only the quality of materials and workmanship. He promised specific temperatures and humidity for given outdoor conditions. The contract even stated tolerances, such as holding 70 degrees F when it was minus 10 degrees F outside, with humidity up to 85 percent (Banham, 1969, chapter 12). To dare make that promise, he had to know both the machine and the environment it served: the moisture load, the activity inside, and, for the first time, the heat from the sun on the structure.

In housing we seldom promise by contract, but we can still ask with numbers. According to Ching (*Building Construction Illustrated*, 2008), thermal comfort depends on four factors: air temperature, relative humidity, the temperature of the surfaces around you, and air movement. For indoor air movement the book recommends roughly 0.05 to 0.25 m/s to avoid drafts. The comfort zones in the book are built on surveys of Americans and Canadians, so treat them as a hint. If a designer tells you the house will be cool, you can ask: cool meaning about how many degrees, in which season, at what hour? That question is mine, not the book's.

## Question 3: do the residents have control?

Banham argues that the goal of environmental science is to find a tolerance zone, not to impose one standard, such as Le Corbusier's 18 degrees C. His measure is the "whole man," neither ideal nor average. The body adapts to short swings, so a system need not answer every degree; sometimes you only need to draw a curtain. I wrote about this in [Build a Tent or Light a Fire](/en/bai-viet/dung-leu-hay-dot-lua/).

Humphreys's adaptive model, as summarized by Hawkes (Bay and Ong, 2006, pp. 37–38), says the same thing: when conditions are uncomfortable, people act to regain comfort. In buildings not forced by machines, occupants tolerate a wider range than in sealed air-conditioned ones. From that come a few design proposals: the environment should be predictable, within the "normal" range of that climate, varied in temperature where appropriate, give anyone who must sit still control close to their body, and avoid sudden temperature changes. The model describes behavior; it does not guarantee that everyone can stand the heat, and tall crowded buildings may still need fans and supplementary air conditioning.

Mahaweli lacked exactly this. The wind was there, but the occupants had no way to adjust it, so they shut it out.

## Question 4: is there a reason to open the window?

Ong and Cam use Singapore's public housing data (Bay and Ong, 2006, chapter 6, pp. 87–97). The blocks of the 1970s were laid out in parallel bars running north to south, excellent thermally, but they sat so close together that residents shut their windows for privacy. Later estates staggered the blocks so that windows do not look straight at each other and face gardens and trees. With a pleasant view, people open the windows, which brings in light and breeze and cuts the electricity bill. Trees outside keep windows open, while asphalt keeps them shut because of glare and heat.

The rule they draw: design the "reasons to open the window," meaning views, privacy, and plants, before thinking about adding more technology. The figures are statistics grouped by flat type and cannot prove cause and effect for any one household, so treat this as a suggestion rather than a law.

## Question 5: has anyone come back to ask?

Mahaweli was never evaluated after occupancy, and that is the biggest gap in its story. In the lecture on building programs (Steinmüller, 2010–2011), the program is no longer a fixed list of rooms but activity over time. UNStudio drew a 24-hour activity section for its 1997 New York competition entry. The lecture has many other examples, but I will mention just this one.

A simple method I came up with, not from any book: three months after moving in, walk around the house and note three things. What has been moved, covered, or blocked. Which spots almost nobody visits. Where people keep gathering. Then ask the designer, or yourself, why.

Tonight, try walking around your house and counting how many things are no longer where they started. Each one answers a question: what is this house actually doing, and what is it being made to do on top of that?
