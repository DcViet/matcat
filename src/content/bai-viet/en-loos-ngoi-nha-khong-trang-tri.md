Picture a wooden tea box with no pattern at all, just a smooth block of wood and a lid. Picking it up, you feel nothing special. Then you open the lid and find a lining of silk inside, deep red and soft under the fingers.

I made this up as an example; it is not a real object. But it is close to what an Austrian architect did with whole houses a century ago: leave the outside plain and care for the inside. His name was Adolf Loos.

## A Lecture About Tattoos

Loos was born in 1870 in Brno and died in 1933 near Vienna. From about 1893 to 1896 he lived in the United States and took whatever manual work came along: mason, floor layer, dishwasher. While there he came across the architecture of the Chicago School and Louis Sullivan's line that form follows function.

In 1910 he gave a lecture in Vienna called "Ornament and Crime." It first appeared in print in French in 1913, and the German version had to wait until 1929. His argument goes like this: when a Papuan covers his body in tattoos, we call it the habit of a young culture, but if a modern European did the same we would call him deviant. In Loos's view, the further a culture advances, the more ornament drains out of everyday things. He added a very practical reason too: ornament goes out of fashion quickly, and craftsmen spend hours carving it, so making it wastes human effort.

*The Fundamentals of Architecture* (AVA, 2007) sums him up more bluntly: all ornament on a building is superfluous. Loos himself was not that extreme. He held only that if there is ornament, it should suit the material, not be stuck on like a coat.

## The House Across from the Palace

Saying it is easy; building it is hard. In 1909 the owners of a tailoring firm, Goldman and Salatsch, hired Loos to build on Michaelerplatz, right in front of the north wing of the Hofburg. It was finished in 1912, and Vienna called it the Looshaus.

The lower floors were shops, clad in green marble from the island of Euboea, with a row of columns. The upper floors were apartments, simply rendered in plaster and punched with rectangular windows. That was all: no moldings, no reliefs. This is why people jokingly called it the "house without eyebrows," because the windows lacked the molded brows above them that every house of the time had.

The press made a fuss in 1910, and one paper called the facade bare in the wrong place. Work went on only after Loos added flower boxes under the windows and defended his design at a public meeting. As for Emperor Franz Joseph, who supposedly disliked it so much that he had the curtains drawn on the palace windows facing the square, that is only a story people repeat, and I could not verify it.

What is worth noticing is what he took away: the things that did no work. The windows, the columns and the stone on the lower floors are all still there.

## Plain Outside, Different Inside

The best place to see Loos is in his houses. Villa Müller in the Střešovice district of Prague, finished in 1930, was designed with Karel Lhota for František and Milada Müller. The owner co-owned a reinforced concrete construction company. From outside, the house is a white plastered box with flat walls and small windows, almost closed off.

Step inside and the story changes. Public rooms are faced in marble, private rooms in wood, with some silk surfaces. More unusual is how he divided the space. Loos called it *Raumplan*, "room plan" or "spatial plan": instead of stacking floors of equal height, he gave each room the height that suited its use. A room that needs to feel generous is raised, a spot that needs to feel snug is lowered. The rooms overlap one another across short flights of steps, so you climb and turn through the house, more like walking a path than a corridor. In one place the floor jumps 1.2 meters.

In 1930 Loos said his architecture did not start from floor plans but from volumes of space. He thought of a room as a block first, and only then as lines on paper.

## So Is Loos Contradicting Himself?

It is a fair question. In *Learning from Las Vegas* (1972), Venturi, Scott Brown and Izenour point out that Modern architects often said one thing and did another. They mention Loos: he scorned ornament but still used beautiful patterns. Kendra Schank Smith's *Architects' Drawings* (2005) likewise records that the Goldman & Salatsch shop and Vienna houses such as the Steiner, Rufer and Scheu houses were simple outside and complex inside.

My way out is to reread the word "ornament" as Loos used it. What he hated was decoration attached to show off, to follow fashion, to pretend to wealth. Stone, wood and the height of a room are not attached; they are the room. That is my own reading, and none of those books says it quite that way.

Smith adds one small detail: a sketch by Loos (p. 125) draws the two towers beside a symmetrical facade larger than life, like a caricature. She reads it as his way of exaggerating to see what needs examining, just like his satirical writing. A man who liked mocking things so much is hardly someone looking for emptiness.

This ties to my piece [Both, Not Just One](/en/bai-viet/venturi-ca-hai-va/): perhaps Loos chose "both plain and rich" without feeling that he was choosing.

## Taking It Home

This part is my own thinking, not in the books. Row houses in Vietnam usually have a single street front, with neighbors tight on both sides, so the facade is often asked to carry too much: showing off wealth, taste, even which floor the family lives on. Loos points another way: let the facade say less, and put the effort where people touch and stay, like door handles, tabletops, stairs, the ceiling height of the living room.

Still, don't read Loos as "minimalism is beautiful." He did not remove richness; he moved it inside. A white house that is also empty inside is only a box.

Tonight, walk once around your home and look for one beautiful thing that nobody tried to decorate: a patch of sun on the stairs, the worn wood of the dining table. Is it beautiful because of how it was shaped, or because someone stuck something onto it?
