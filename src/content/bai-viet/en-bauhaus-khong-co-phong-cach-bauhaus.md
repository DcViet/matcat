This example is mine, not a real memory. You walk into a furniture store and see a sign reading "Bauhaus style" hanging over a tubular steel chair and a round lamp. The salesperson is quite sure: Bauhaus means straight lines, square blocks, white paint and metal.

If the man who ran the Bauhaus school in 1929 heard that, he would probably wince. In the first issue of the journal *Bauhaus* that year, he wrote plainly that they were not looking for a Bauhaus style, and not chasing a Bauhaus fashion.

## Who Wrote That Line

Hannes Meyer, born in Basel in 1889, succeeded Walter Gropius as director of the Bauhaus in Dessau in April 1928. He was an architect, and at that time he was building the German trade union federation's school at Bernau with Hans Wittwer. In 1929-30 he also extended the Törten housing estate, also in Dessau, with a row of houses with exterior access galleries.

In August 1930 he left the director's chair. According to Cohen, Mies van der Rohe was appointed to replace him on August 5, 1930; I touch on Mies in [Neue Nationalgalerie: A Big Steel Roof and a Nearly Empty Room](/en/cong-trinh/neue-nationalgalerie-mies/). That is why this issue is worth reading: it records the voice of the Bauhaus just before the school changed direction.

## Bauhaus as a Social Matter

Meyer's essay is called "Bauhaus und Gesellschaft," "Bauhaus and Society." He opens with the idea that right design is a form of organizing existence. Then he writes that the Bauhaus in Dessau is not an artistic phenomenon but a social one (pp. 2-3).

From there, the designer's task is handed down by society, not by personal taste. He lists a great deal to be done: schools, gardens and houses for the people, hundreds of thousands of homes for ordinary families, millions of pieces of affordable furniture. The designer serves the community, so he must observe the life and spirit of the people who will use what he makes.

The word "people" (Volk) in the essay carries the overtones of the 1920s, so I do not read it in its later sense.

## Art Is Order

Meyer does not drop the word art, he changes its meaning. For him, art is not for making things pretty and is not an emotional act; it is only order. Each age has its own kind of order: the classical age had the module of Euclidean space, the Gothic age had sharp angles, the Renaissance had the golden ratio. His own time needed a new objective order, for everyone (pp. 2-3).

Understood this way, art theory becomes a set of ordering rules that every designer needs. The "artist" is no longer a trade; it becomes a calling to arrange things tidily.

## The Workshop and Respect for Materials

In the same issue, Lu Märten brings historical materialism into the question of form. She argues that form does not come from the thought "let us make art." It comes from materials, tools, economic conditions and the purpose of living. So the workshop (Werkstatt) is the ground of new creation: it respects the material and also the purpose it serves, and it accepts experiment, because every material has its own laws. Paper teaches a different sense of form than iron does (pp. 4-9).

This part of the journal is hard to read, so I keep only the general idea. That idea is clear enough: if you want form, start from your hands and your materials.

## A Lamp and an Argument

The word "style" was also argued over inside this very issue. Naum Gabo claimed that the Bauhaus wanted to create a style, and used the metal workshop's spherical lamp as his example: they chose the sphere and the cylinder simply because they loved them. Marianne Brandt replied that the lamp came from the curve of light, from economy, from experiment and calculation, though some feeling remained. They did not invent the light bulb; they looked for a way to use it that suited its nature, rather than copying the shape of a candle or an oil lamp.

Gabo answered again: the Bauhaus globe lamp copies an existing frosted bulb on the market, and adds a layer of glass that darkens it. In his view, only thinking about form, and therefore about style, produces mistakes (p. 21).

I do not take a side, because both saw the same danger: anything born from a real reason is easily copied by later people into a mere look.

## The Minimum Dwelling, and a Warning About Opium

Ernst Kállai wrote "Wir leben nicht, um zu wohnen," meaning we do not live in order to dwell; we dwell in order to live. He cites Mart Stam: drop the idea of the ideal house and ideal furnishings, and move toward the minimum dwelling (Minimalwohnung). Furniture can stay simple, beautiful or not does not matter, and housework should take up the smallest part of a life (pp. 10-14, 20-25).

He also warns directly about a line of Le Corbusier's, the one saying that solving the housing problem will bring society back into balance. Kállai calls that argument very suspect, because it can make things comfortable for capital and the state: a worker with a small machine for living, a radio and a vegetable plot will not think "foolish thoughts." In his words, not only religion and art, but housing too can be an opium. I write about Le Corbusier in [A House Is a Machine for Living In: The Question Before the Shape](/en/bai-viet/le-corbusier-huong-toi-mot-kien-truc-moi/).

## Three Voices, One Question

| Voice | What they stress |
|---|---|
| Meyer | Bauhaus is a social phenomenon; housing for ordinary people; art is order |
| Märten | Form comes out of materials, the workshop and the purpose of life |
| Kállai | The minimum dwelling; housing must not become opium |

![Two ways to start a house: from a look, or from life](/images/bauhaus-khong-co-phong-cach-bauhaus-en.svg)

The three speak differently but ask the same question: who is this house for, made of what, under what conditions? The Bernau school drawing on the cover answers in that direction. On the sheet are floor plans, window-frame details, the frame of the building, and a narrow column of ways to arrange desks for different kinds of sessions. The shape of the building is the result of those answers.

## What Came After

Meyer, Märten and Kállai wanted to shut style out the door, and yet the Bauhaus became a style anyway, now sitting on a sign in a store. What the writers intended and what people remembered afterward can go in two different directions, so when I read this issue I treat it as one voice of one moment.

It is also a polemical journal, written when the Bauhaus leaned hard toward the social. It does not represent the whole school, since before Meyer came Gropius and after Meyer came Mies.

Try one small thing. Next time you see an object with a label like "this style or that style," ask yourself three questions: who uses it, what is it made of, and if you took the look away, what job could it still do. If you find it interesting, tell me about the object.
