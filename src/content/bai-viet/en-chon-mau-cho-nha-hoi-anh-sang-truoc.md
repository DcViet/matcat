Picture this. You carry a warm gray paint swatch home, hold it against the living room wall, and it looks a little blue. By evening the same swatch has turned yellowish. This example is mine, not anyone's real story, but almost everyone who has painted a room has met some version of it.

It happens because we tend to think color belongs to the wall. The books I read say the opposite: color belongs to the light that falls on the wall. So the first question when choosing colors for a home is not "what color do I like?" but "what kind of light does this room receive?"

## We only see the colors the light contains

According to Gordon (*Interior Lighting for Designers*, 2003), color is not a property of an object. It results from the wavelengths an object reflects or lets through, and we can only see colors that exist in the light source. His example is a tomato: red under ordinary light, but dark gray under a green lamp (ch. 4).

Every light source has its own "color temperature," measured in kelvins. Around 2800 K is a warm yellowish white, like an incandescent bulb. Around 5000 K is white like daylight. Up at 8000 K it leans bluish white. Light rich in red makes skin look healthy and pink, a cool source makes it look pale, and your walls obey the same rule.

So a paint sample has to be tested under the kind of lamp you will actually use. Gordon's advice: if you do not yet know which lamp you will install, test under two sources, one leaning blue (daylight fluorescent) and one leaning red (incandescent). He also flags an easy mistake. The color rendering index (CRI) can only be compared between lamps whose color temperatures are close, within roughly 100 to 300 K. A 3000 K lamp and a 6500 K lamp with the same CRI of 75 still render colors differently (pp. 45–50). I wrote about what good light is in [Good Light Is Not Plenty of Light](/en/bai-viet/anh-sang-tot-khong-phai-anh-sang-nhieu/).

## Which way does your room face?

Matthew Frederick (*101 Things I Learned in Architecture School*, 2007) sums up light by compass direction, for the Northern Hemisphere. North light is almost shadowless, even, and neutral. East light is strongest in the morning, low, with soft long shadows and a yellowish gray tone. South light is plentiful from late morning to late afternoon and tends to pick up color from its surroundings. West light is strong in late afternoon, a deep orange-yellow, and easily harsh.

Rasmussen (*Experiencing Architecture*, 1959, chapter IX) adds a very practical point. A north-facing room receives only skylight, which is slightly blue, so cool colors glow there while warm colors look ugly, as if seen under a blue lamp. Use cool colors in north rooms and warm colors in south rooms and every color comes alive. To show that both approaches are right, he sets Vermeer beside Pieter de Hooch. Vermeer painted north-facing rooms in cool tones, pale blue and lemon yellow. De Hooch painted west-facing rooms in warm tones, brown and vermilion. Both are beautiful. The cover of this piece is a Vermeer, so look at how the blue and the yellow sit together in it.

One caution from my own notes: all of this applies to temperate latitudes. In Vietnam the sun climbs high and passes close to overhead, so shading from the sun and catching the breeze matter more than "tinting" a room to suit its direction.

## Color changes how a room feels

Nutsch's handbook (*Basic Rules of Furniture and Interior Design*, 2014) shows in diagrams how the color of each surface shifts our sense of a room's size.

![Four rooms, each with one dark surface: ceiling, end wall, side walls, floor, with the effect we perceive](/images/chon-mau-cho-nha-hoi-anh-sang-truoc-en.svg)

- A dark ceiling makes the room lower and tighter.
- A dark end wall makes the room shorter.
- Two dark side walls facing each other make the room taller and deeper.
- Light walls make the room feel wider.
- A dark floor feels solid; a light floor feels slippery and stimulating.
- Dark color on every surface feels confining.

Lines matter too. Vertical lines on the end wall make the room taller, and horizontal lines on the side walls make it look wider. Ching (*A Visual Dictionary of Architecture*, 2012, pp. 30–31) says warm colors such as red, orange, and yellow seem to advance toward the viewer, while green, blue, and violet recede. To pull something closer, use warm color. To make a space look larger, use cool color.

Rasmussen does not fully agree. He thinks this kind of "camouflage" can backfire. In his view, a small room should be painted dark so that its occupants feel the four walls embracing them, and a large room should be painted light so that its spaciousness shows. I think the two views meet at one point: test under real light before deciding. These conventions are Western visual conventions, and the results depend on light and culture.

## Color lives in materials, and next to other colors

Rasmussen points out that color is tied to material and weave: with the same fiber, glossy satin differs from thick velvet. In the beginning a house took its color from local materials such as mud walls, stone, and straw. In Siena the walls were plastered in the local earth color. He also warns about two common surprises. A color that looks wonderful on one wall can lose its appeal when copied into another room. And gray placed beside red turns bluish.

Nutsch discusses the same thing for furniture and interiors (ch. 5–7, pp. 13–20). Wood is warm and lively, metal is cold, glass sits in between, and cold stainless steel does not suit a room meant to feel comfortable. His method is to choose materials by contrast and then pick one overall tone for the whole set, like harmony in a piece of music. One detail that sticks out too much is a wrong note. The parts of the book that describe each color as "cheerful," "hot," or "mysterious" are Western convention, not experimental results, so do not treat them as laws.

With that in mind, when you choose a wood floor, curtains, or a cabinet, do not choose each item alone. Lay the samples next to each other, right up against the wall color, under the lamp you will use.

## Use color like Barragán: attach it to objects

In [Barragán: A Wall, Color and a Patch of Sky in a Closed Courtyard](/en/cong-trinh/barragan-mau-va-anh-sang/) I wrote about how he used color. According to the thesis on Casa Cristo, Barragán did not spread color evenly over everything. Color attached to gates, iron bars, and structure, and plants were also grown in color groups (pp. 18–20, 23). The source gives no specific hues, so I will not claim which shades he used. To know that, you would need photographs and primary documents.

For an ordinary house, the idea can become choosing one or two colored pieces, such as a door, a cabinet, or a chair, and letting the walls play the background.

## No rule guarantees anything

Rasmussen rejects rigid rules. He mentions a German theorist who proposed an earth-colored floor, multicolored walls, and a light ceiling, then says that the blue carpet in his own house does not make him uneasy at all. In his view, no rule can guarantee good architecture.

So the honest way is to test. Stick three paint samples in three different places in the room, each one big enough, not just a small square. Look in the morning, at noon, and in the evening, and under the lamp you actually use, placed against the floor, the door, and the wood furniture. I made up this method myself; it does not come from any book.

Tonight, try turning off the ceiling light, switching on a desk lamp, and looking at the living room wall again. Is it still the color you chose?
