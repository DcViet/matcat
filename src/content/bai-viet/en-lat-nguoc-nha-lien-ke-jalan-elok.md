This example is one I made up, not anyone's memory. Picture a town house with a front about five or six meters wide and shared walls on both sides. You push open the door: living room up front, stairs in the middle, kitchen at the very back. At noon, both ends of the house get some daylight, but the middle needs a lamp. The smell of braised fish downstairs crawls up the stairs to the top floor.

Plenty of narrow town houses are laid out this way, because everyone assumes guests sit in front and the kitchen goes behind. In Singapore, an architect tried flipping that whole order. I'll describe the house, then set it beside the Vietnamese tube house to see what each can teach the other.

## What the Jalan Elok House Does

According to *Singapore Houses* (Robert Powell and Albert Lim, 2009, pp. 84-91), it is No. 19 in a row of terrace houses near Orchard Road, renovated by Chang Yong Ter. A typical terrace house there is about 6 m wide: living room in front, stairs in the middle, then dining, kitchen, and the helper's room at the back. Chang recast it as three zones across and four zones deep. The middle zone is 4 m wide. On each side is a slot for light and air, 1 m wide.

Then he swapped the order. An open kitchen sits right at the entrance, behind the car porch, with no wall in front; the first layer of security is a sliding steel-wire screen. The kitchen becomes the place for receiving guests, and the helper can see who is arriving. The living room is squeezed between two green walls. Its back wall is gravel set in cement, so rainwater runs down into a shallow pond. An open stair runs along the party wall through all four floors. Overhead, a skylight cuts through every level, with a sliding roof trellis covered in climbing plants as a canopy. Chang calls the house "upside down and inside out." The owner valued volume over floor area.

## Why This Layout Feels Airy

Bawa, as Powell recorded from a 1994 conversation, had ten criteria for tropical houses. The ninth is a plan only one room deep, open on two opposite sides so the breeze can pass through (p. 10). I wrote about him in [Geoffrey Bawa: A Tropical House Is a Place to Pass Through and Stay](/en/cong-trinh/bawa-nha-nhiet-doi-di-xuyen-qua/). A terrace house hits a wall right here: it has only one street face and cannot open on two sides. The answer is to make the courtyard and skylight a "second face." Powell also admits that an urban house "cannot be that pure."

The skylight does one more job. Powell notes that a few Singapore houses use a wind shaft, a vertical void through several floors with an air opening at the top. Warm air is lighter, so it rises and draws fresh air in from below (p. 16). Jalan Elok has two 1 m slots and a skylight through every floor, so it belongs in that group. But there is a warning too: some argue that rooftop ventilation tricks like towers and roof vents are almost useless in the tropics, because the air outside is already hot and humid. I read the two views as not fully in conflict: a wind shaft adds to crosswind and pulls out hot air when the wind is still. Its real effect depends on the temperature difference in each house, and you have to measure it to know.

As for numbers, Tan and Robson quote a recommended floor depth of about 13.5 to 15 m for cross ventilation without drafts, and a distance of 6 to 12 m from window to core for daylight all day. The book says these need checking, so I treat them only as hints. Whether light is plentiful or poor is also not as simple as counting meters. I talk about that in [Good Light Is Not Plenty of Light](/en/bai-viet/anh-sang-tot-khong-phai-anh-sang-nhieu/).

## Where the Vietnamese Tube House Stands

Hanoi's Old Quarter tube houses, according to Bay and Ong, are 2.5 to 5.5 m wide and sometimes more than 60 m deep, in a district of about 1,000 people per hectare. Courtyards once handled light and air, then were slowly filled in by extra building. With too little floor area, people run more air conditioning, and the grid overloads in summer.

In 2003, a Japanese-Vietnamese research project (the University of Tokyo and Hanoi University of Civil Engineering) built a model of new housing for the 36-streets district. Architect Kazuhiro Kojima designed it, and chapter 12 of *Tropical Sustainable Architecture* (Bay and Ong, 2006) retells it. The house keeps the original plot and opens only to the street, alternates solid blocks with voids in a rhythm, and links the inner courtyards vertically into wind corridors. Because of an ownership dispute, it had to be built on the university campus.

Both are lived in from front to back, and the street face is a layer of security and exchange. They differ on one big point: the Jalan Elok owner was glad to give up floor area, while Hanoi needs more of it.

## Bringing It Home

That difference decides how much you can borrow. This part is my own idea, and nobody has verified it. If you want to keep floor area at a maximum, you may not need a whole 4 m zone. You could keep one 1 m slot in the Jalan Elok manner as a through-floor gap, fused with the stair and corridor. The rooms stay whole, and the dark middle of the house still gets a path for light and air to come down.

A few cautions are in order. The two houses sit in different settings: one is a family house on its own land, expensive, the other is the old quarter with many households packed together. Many numbers in the books come from equatorial climates. I think Hanoi also needs a line for cold and damp, while Ho Chi Minh City is closer to the conditions in the book.

Try one small thing. Take a sheet of paper and draw your home's plan as a long, narrow strip, then shade the spots where you still need a lamp at noon. Then ask yourself: if you gave up one meter of width for a slot of sky, what would you lose, and what would that dark stretch gain?
