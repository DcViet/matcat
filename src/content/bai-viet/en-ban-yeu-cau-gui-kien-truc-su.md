Picture a couple who go to see an architect with one sentence: "A three-storey house, three bedrooms, a big kitchen, and airy." This is my own example, not a real story. Three weeks later they receive the drawings. The living room sits in the darkest corner, the kitchen is closed like a box, and the third bedroom is just big enough for one bed. Nobody did anything wrong. The first sentence held only that much, and the architect filled the gaps with the most reasonable guesses they had.

A brief is a sheet of paper that writes those gaps down before anyone draws. It is not long, it is not a legal document, and both sides only need to be able to read it.

## One Sheet of Paper Both Sides Can Read

Louis Kahn had an idea that I think applies to houses as well. In *Essential Texts*, edited by Twombly (2003), the first move is to turn an area into a space. The client gives a number. The architect gives back a place to live, to meet, to walk through.

Kahn had a much larger example. Jonas Salk gave him the floor area for a research institute, with one more wish: to invite Picasso. What came out of that were things no area table lists: a meeting room, an unnamed lobby, and research rooms set apart so that scientists had room to think. A house has no research board, but the reason is the same. A floor area cannot tell you how you live. A brief can.

## Five Things to Include

A brief can be split into five parts. Each part answers one question, and the answer should be specific enough that a stranger could picture it.

| Part | The question to answer | My example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Daily needs | In a day, what happens in the house, where, and when? | The whole family eats together in the morning. Dad reads alone until late at night. |
| Budget and scope | Does the number include construction, furniture, the garden, plumbing and wiring? | A number without furniture and a number with furniture are two different problems. |
| Site and orientation | Dimensions, levels, where the sun and wind come from, noise, the neighbouring houses. | The plot opens to the west, so the front faces hard afternoon sun. |
| How you live in the house | Who lives there, who works from home, who needs quiet, who likes company. | Mum works from home and needs a corner away from the television. |
| What you do not want | Things you fear, or things you have seen in other people's homes and disliked. | No bedroom window facing straight into the neighbour's house. |

The examples in the table are mine, to show how such lines might read. Your household will have different answers.

## Daily Needs: Ask What Happens, Not Only How Many Rooms

In *101 Things I Learned in Architecture School* (2007), Matthew Frederick advises against drawing a rectangle and then naming it for a use (item 12, p. 15). Find out what will actually happen in the space, imagine the situations, and let the architecture answer them.

For a brief, this means that instead of "bedroom for three," you write "a place for the child to study in the evening, and a place where Grandpa can sleep when the kitchen is still being cleaned." Those two sentences tell the designer where to put the door, how to block sound, and how much light to bring in. A room count tells them none of that.

## Site and Orientation: Measure First, Do Not Guess

A brief needs numbers. More important is knowing which numbers were measured and which were guessed. A habit that helps during a site survey is to tag each figure: measured, cross-checked, supplied by the owner, assumed, unknown, or needs a specialist. A guess such as "about six metres" is still useful, but it should not look like a measurement on the drawing.

Beyond dimensions, write down the things that are hard to measure but shape the house: where the neighbour's windows look, when the lane is noisy, whether rain blows onto the porch, which rooms get afternoon sun. These sound small. They decide where the windows go and how deep the eave should be.

## Budget: Say What the Number Covers

Budgets often arrive as a bare figure. Nobody knows whether it includes furniture, the gate and the fence, or the concealed electrical and plumbing work. Two people who both say "our budget is a certain figure" may be talking about very different houses. Writing down what is in and out, and how much room there is to move, takes a few minutes and saves many rounds of revision later.

## Unwanted Things and Real Limits

Unwanted things rarely appear in a client's brief, yet they are the most useful thing a designer can get. Limits belong on the list too: a steep plot, a narrow plot, an old wall you want to keep. Write them down from the start.

Frederick, writing about constraints (items 97-98, pp. 75-76), argues that a constraint can be material. A steep site that is hard to build on in the usual way can open a vertical connection through stairs or a ramp with a clear point of interest. An ugly old wall can become a frame for a view. Writing a limit down does not lose the plot. It gives the designer something to work with, instead of something to avoid.

## From a Style Word to an Argument

Many people write "minimal," "warm," or "Japanese style." These words are not wrong, but on their own they do not settle any decision. A good concept is an argument. It connects the brief to the site, and it helps the designer answer concrete questions: where the window goes, whether the walls are thick or thin, whether the floor is stone or timber.

You can ask the architect one simple question: "When we say minimal, what are we leaving out?" The answer will show how far they have thought it through. If the reply is a beautiful photograph with nothing said about what goes, that is the moment to ask more.

## How to Write It: Short, Specific, Honest About Gaps

Two or three pages are enough, as long as every line holds an answer. Do not be shy about leaving gaps, but mark them as unknown so the designer knows what to ask again.

Many practices use a survey form at the first meeting with a client. A good way to fill it in is for both sides to fill it together, leave blank whatever is not yet clear, and come back to it later. Attach a few pictures you like and a few you dislike, each with a short sentence explaining why. The pictures you dislike often say more than the ones you like.

Put the date at the top. When the family changes, an old brief will show what you needed at that time.

## Why Writing It Down Helps Both Sides

For the architect, a clear brief means fewer rounds of redrawing caused by misunderstanding. For the homeowner, it is a measure. When the drawings arrive, you can check each line against what you wrote and understand why the living room sits where it does, and why the stair turns toward the back. People tend to remember a feeling about the house they want. A sheet of paper keeps the numbers, the habits, and the things to avoid. Each one supports the other.

You can try it tonight. Write the five parts above, three to five sentences each, in about half an hour. Then read it aloud. Wherever you stumble, you have probably not thought the point through yet, and that is the place to ask more when you meet the architect.
