This example is mine, not a true story. You walk into a friend's living room on a sticky summer afternoon. Nobody has turned on a fan, yet the room is cool. You sit down on the seat by the window, and it is oddly warm even though the sun is out. You can't say why, only that it feels good.

Now move the scene to 1908, a suburb of Chicago, where winters bite and summers are hot and damp. The cool room and the warm seat are no longer imaginary, because Banham describes exactly that in a living room Frank Lloyd Wright designed.

## What people usually say about Prairie houses

When Wright comes up, the familiar points follow: the house sits low, the horizontal lines stretch out, the gently sloped roof reaches far past the walls. The cover photo of this piece shows the Robie House in Chicago, and you can see all of it: thin bricks running sideways, flat stone slabs on top of the walls, an upper floor sheltered under a long roof.

Steen Eiler Rasmussen, in *Experiencing Architecture* (1959, chapter III), reads Wright through the eye. At Fallingwater (Bear Run, Pennsylvania), he sees the contrast between rough stone and smooth concrete, and horizontal volumes wrapped around the rock of the waterfall. He also points out that the famous photograph makes the house look dramatic; once the trees are in leaf, you only see light horizontal lines between the trunks, and the house feels close, almost part of the valley.

That is a true and lovely reading. But Reyner Banham, in *The Architecture of the Well-Tempered Environment* (1969), asks a different question: how does the house live through a whole year?

## The Baker House living room, measured with a ruler and a thermometer

The Baker House in Wilmette, Illinois, is a fairly light frame of timber and plaster. Banham dates it 1908; other sources on the house say it was finished in 1909, so I write "around 1908 to 1909." A house that light still gets through an Illinois winter and a hot summer, thanks to the way its parts work together (chapter 6).

The numbers, following Banham: the living room is 32 ft long, 17 ft wide and about 14 ft high, roughly 9.8 by 5.2 by 4.3 meters. A gently sloped roof reaches south and shades a bay window 5 ft by 12 ft, with a seat and planters inside. Under the roof runs a band of glass about 2 ft high around three sides of the room. Banham argues that this band is what actually carries light deep inside.

The best part is the heating. The fireplace at the end of the room, he says, is only a symbol. The room is really heated by hot-water pipes hidden in the baseboard, running to a large radiator under the bay window seat, which has slots to let the warmth out. The heat is proportional to the heat lost through the glass, so the coldest spots get the most. In summer it works the other way: the bay opens almost completely, and two small windows high at the end of the room can be reached only from a small gallery above the fireplace. The gallery looks like a perch for musicians and seems useless, but it lets hot air escape at the highest point and sends a cross draft through the room.

![Five details of the Baker House living room and the jobs each one does](/images/wright-nha-lien-voi-dat-en.svg)

Look at the diagram and you will see that nothing does just one job. The seat is for sitting and hides the heater. The gallery is a gallery and also the way to reach the vent. Banham draws two lessons: services should work together with the structure, each detail doing several jobs; and this performance needed no new technology.

## Other houses do the same

Banham lists more Wright houses to show this was a habit, not an accident. At the Gale House, radiators sit inside hollow "supports." At the Robie House, the north court is cool like a pool of cold air, and the eaves cast shade at the right hours. Almost every opening has a heater beside it. The Ross cottage (1902) opens on three sides for cross breezes, and 84 percent of the living room walls are windows.

Banham also sets Wright beside California houses of the same years to compare ways of keeping out heat. Irving Gill used heavy concrete walls close to adobe. Maybeck used deep eaves and pergolas. Greene and Greene pushed the roof to the limit at the Gamble House (Pasadena, 1908), where the sleeping porches and the covered court take up nearly as much area as the interior. Behrens's own house in Darmstadt, the same year, is still a cube cut into smaller boxes. By Banham's account, Wright's advantages were an extreme climate, light construction, skilled builders, intelligent clients, and above all genius.

There is a relative in another Wright building. The Larkin Building in Buffalo (1906) is a "sealed brick cliff," and four stair towers at the corners serve as both exits and air intakes. Banham counts it a masterpiece of environmentally conditioned architecture, even though its exterior can lead historians to mistake it for simplified classicism.

## Why this is worth remembering

If you have read [Build a Tent or Light a Fire](/en/bai-viet/dung-leu-hay-dot-lua/), you remember Banham's question: given firewood, do you build a shelter or light a fire? Wright does both, and does it well. The deep eaves and the glass band are the "tent"; the hot-water pipes hidden under the seat are the "fire." The two meet at the sketch stage.

It is fair to name the limits. This way of heating worked because fuel was cheap; today we have to count consumption. Banham also suspects the Robie House is hard to heat, partly because a floor radiator may never have been installed. And Rasmussen is not wrong: reading a house with the eye is still a valid way to read it, just not a complete one.

## Try it yourself

Next time you step into a room that feels good, ask three small questions. Where does the light come in from, and how deep does it go? Where does the air leave? And which part of the room is quietly doing two or three jobs at once? You may find a "Wright seat" in your own home.
