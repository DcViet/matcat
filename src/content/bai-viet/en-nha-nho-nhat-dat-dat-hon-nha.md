Here is an example I made up. A couple buys a plot four and a half meters wide in a crowded neighborhood. They sit down with an architect and ask the usual question: "How long will this house last?" In many places people hope to hear "three or four generations." In Japan the true answer is blunter, and it changes the way people build.

In *Small Houses: Contemporary Japanese Dwellings* (Claudia Hildner, 2011), the introduction and Ulf Meyer's essay say that the average Japanese house lasts only about 25 years before it is demolished and rebuilt. The real value sits in the plot (pp. 7-13). It sounds harsh, but the consequence is interesting: clients do not treat the house as something to keep forever, so they are willing to accept something strange.

## Why Small Houses Became Laboratories

Hildner uses "small houses" for private homes that stand out for their space and design. Architects in Japan can try odd things fairly easily for a few reasons that add up: there are almost no design guidelines, clients know what they are hiring and will change their habits, and the house will be replaced sooner or later anyway. For many young architects, one small house is the only chance to be noticed. At the "Tokyo Metabolizing" exhibition in Venice in 2010, the small houses were presented as the liveliest part of a city that never stops changing.

Let me look at three experiments, each answering a different question.

## Experiment One: The House Is Just a Staircase

Sou Fujimoto's Final Wooden House (Kumamoto, 2008) is the most extreme. Hildner gives the plot as 4.2 x 4.2 m. The structure, the outer skin, and the interior are all made of square cedar beams stacked in a staggered pattern into steps, each step a multiple of 35 cm high (pp. 56-59). One step is a seat, two steps are a tabletop, three steps are a place to stand and work. So the house has no furniture: solid and void fit into each other like yin and yang.

![Four 35 cm steps next to a 1.7 m person: one step as a seat, two as a table, three as a standing desk](/images/nha-nho-nhat-dat-dat-hon-nha-en.svg)

Look at the drawing and you will see how ordinary those numbers are. Thirty-five centimeters is the height of a chair, seventy is a dining table, a little over a meter is a kitchen counter. Fujimoto adds nothing else; he just divides height into steps that fit a person.

House H (also by Fujimoto, Tokyo, 2009) goes another way. It imitates a young tree that branches, with many levels joined by steps, almost filling the plot. Large openings through walls and floors let the whole house hear itself (pp. 122-125). Tower Machiya by Atelier Bow-Wow (Tokyo, 2010) stands the machiya, the traditional wooden town house, up into a tower on a plot only the size of a parking space. The stair replaces the old machiya's corridor: it winds like a garden path through four staggered floors, with a seat on the second floor, and ends at a tea room (pp. 150-153).

## Experiment Two: The House Turns Its Back on the Street

In Japan the code sets a maximum height by the width of the street, and adds a slanted line on the north side so neighbors still get sun. Add fire and earthquake rules, and the result is gaps between houses and upper floors cut at an angle. Meyer describes the city as a patchwork with little context. Many architects therefore take a defensive stance and separate the house from the street (pp. 11-23).

The best known is Tadao Ando's Sumiyoshi Nagaya (1976). He took the nagaya, the Edo-period row house, as his reference. According to the book, the plot is about 3.5 x 14 m. The street side is a blank concrete wall, 6 m high, cut by a single slit for the door. Inside, the rooms gather around a courtyard open to the sky and as wide as the house, so to get from one room to the one behind, you have to cross the yard. The same year, Toyo Ito built White U for his widowed sister: bare concrete in a U shape, a long corridor, a protected yard. It was demolished in 1997. I wrote about light inside a closed concrete box like this in [The Church of the Light: A Dark Room with Just One Slit of Light](/en/cong-trinh/nha-tho-anh-sang-ando/).

## Experiment Three: The Floor Is Furniture, the Step Is Rank

The traditional Japanese house is a modular timber frame, with sliding fusuma doors, tatami mats (about 80 x 182 cm, varying by region), and paper shoji screens. Bedding is rolled up and put away during the day, so there is no need for a bed, chairs, or a high table. The floor does all those jobs itself. Its height and material also tell you which room is formal and which is casual (pp. 15, 79, 127), and the place to take off shoes, the genkan, is still there today.

Seen this way, Fujimoto's 35 cm step is not a trick that fell from the sky. He took an old idea, "the floor is furniture," and tipped it into the vertical.

## What a Vietnamese Town House Can Take From This

I think three questions are worth bringing home, not three shapes.

- **Do you need floor area, or volume?** All three houses give up floor area to gain height, a yard, or space that runs through several floors. In [A Narrow Terrace House That Is Still Bright: Jalan Elok Turns the Plan Inside Out](/en/bai-viet/lat-nguoc-nha-lien-ke-jalan-elok/), one owner does exactly that.
- **What else can the stair do?** In Tower Machiya it is a corridor, a garden, a place to sit. In our narrow town houses, the stair usually only goes up.
- **Which side do you close, and which do you open?** Ando closed the street side and opened to the sky. The Vietnamese tube house also has just one street face, so the question is identical.

For the tube house, the real lesson is experimenting with a plan. Kazuhiro Kojima proposed stacking cubes on one module, each cube equal to one tube-house unit, into a "porous structure" with vertical courtyards for air and light. One of his test houses was built at the Hanoi University of Civil Engineering, finished in September 2003, with a reinforced concrete frame and plastered brick painted white, like an ordinary Vietnamese house (Bay and Ong, 2006, pp. 228-240). He tested on a real house, at close to an average price, something anyone could build, rather than putting up a strange object.

A few cautions are in order. The book presents these experiments as good ideas and does not discuss maintenance, small children, older people, or a humid climate. The Final Wooden House sits in a private garden and is not a typical permanent home. And the 25-year life of Japanese houses hardly translates to us: Vietnamese homes are usually long-term assets that several generations share. To me that means experiments here should be slower and quieter, for example trying one corner of the stair before trying the whole house.

Try one small thing. Measure the height of the steps in your own home, then ask yourself: which step is already high enough to sit on, and where could two more steps turn into a table?
