Picture a small courtyard in the afternoon. In the middle sits a stone trough, and water trickles steadily from a tap into it. Out in the alley motorbikes keep passing, but standing in the courtyard you hear the water first and the traffic falls behind it. The courtyard is exactly as loud as before, yet it feels calmer. This example is mine, not anyone's memory. It leads me to a line that sounds backward: silence sings.

The line belongs to Luis Barragán. In his 1980 Pritzker Prize speech he said that in his fountains, silence sings (the official English transcript from the Pritzker Prize reads "in my fountains, silence sings"). I covered his color and his walls in [Barragán: A Wall, Color and a Patch of Sky in a Closed Courtyard](/en/cong-trinh/barragan-mau-va-anh-sang/). This time I only follow the water.

## Water at the Alhambra

The Alhambra is the palace-fortress of the Nasrid dynasty in Granada, built mostly in the 13th and 14th centuries. The book *Al-Andalus: The Art of Islamic Spain* (the catalogue of the Met's 1992 exhibition) has a whole chapter on the Alhambra gardens, by D. Fairchild Ruggles. According to the book, the gardens are tied to water, courtyards, shade, and views, and in how architecture is read here, light, water, and shade are all materials. The inner courts have pools.

The two best-known courts are the Court of the Myrtles and the Court of the Lions. The Myrtles was completed under Yusuf I (1333 to 1354) and refurbished later by Muhammad V. The Court of the Lions was built by Muhammad V between 1362 and 1391. The book treats them as the most refined point of the whole palace. You also do not step straight into a court. The usual route runs like this: enter the Mexuar, pass through the Comares Palace, and only then reach the Court of the Lions. A court is where you arrive after a sequence of spaces, not where you stand at the door.

Here I only read it through the example at the top: a still pool and a small jet of water make a courtyard's silence easier to notice. That is how I understand it, not something the book says.

## Barragán brought water from two places

A thesis on Casa Cristo (author unknown) argues that Barragán's philosophy grew from two experiences. The first is the Los Corrales ranch in Jalisco, where he spent his childhood. He remembered weirs, cisterns, the well in a monastery courtyard, troughs for cattle, small reflecting ponds, and old aqueducts. He said they followed him whether he was awake or asleep (thesis, pp. 16–17).

The second is his European trip from May 1924, with the Alhambra and Generalife. At Generalife he saw water channels and fountains, and that he recognized Moorish traits in how he handled water and gardens. With a group of friends in Guadalajara, he reworked the Mediterranean and Moorish spirit for Mexico (pp. 9–26).

Both places feed the same thing: water and the wall are his two signatures. I arranged the table below to make the comparison easier to see, and added nothing.

|---|---|---|
| Where water appears | inner courts with pools; at Generalife, channels and fountains | one small channel, one trough, one reflecting basin |
| What goes with it | courtyards, shade, views | the wall |

## One channel, one basin, one flow

Barragán's water is very sparing. It comes down to three strokes: one channel, one basin, one flow, usually beside a wall. In the Pritzker speech he said that in his gardens and houses he always tried to keep the soft whisper of silence inside, and that people have both a need and a duty to make serene places that pass that feeling on to others.

I read this as a division of labor. The wall holds the street out. In the emptiness the wall leaves behind, water steps in, either as a small running sound or as a still surface that mirrors the sky. That division of labor is my idea. The use is clearer: the sound of water covers some of the noise, a water surface cools the eye, and a long run of water can pull a space into an axis.

It is very different from Ryoan-ji, the gravel courtyard I wrote about in [Ryoan-ji: Three Ways to Read a Gravel Courtyard](/en/nghe-thuat/ryoan-ji-ba-cach-doc/). There is no drop of water there, and the gravel only suggests the sea. Barragán puts real water in, but very little of it.

## The water channel at the Salk plaza

The clearest example is at the Salk Institute. According to Antonio Amado Lorenzo’s article (Revista EGA), Louis Kahn had first imagined a garden of trees there, and in early 1965 he phoned and wrote to ask Barragán for advice. In early 1966 Barragán came to La Jolla. He touched the concrete wall and said not to put a single tree or blade of grass there: this should be a plaza of stone, and it “will have a facade toward the sky.” Then he sketched a water channel more than 60 m long, pointing toward the sea (pp. 131–132). For more on Kahn and how he works with light, see [Kimbell: Making the Sun Take a Detour Before It Touches the Paintings](/en/cong-trinh/kimbell-kahn/).

Here water is no longer an object placed in a courtyard. It becomes a line poured into the ocean, leading the eye of anyone standing on the plaza toward the horizon. The article's author also reads the scheme through two other influences: Le Corbusier's idea of the "framed landscape" and the metaphysical paintings of de Chirico, with their empty spaces and symmetrical axes. I note this to be complete, because it shows that Barragán's water does not come only from the Alhambra.

One practical point: water needs care. Moss and mosquitoes will come if nobody looks after it.

## Try it yourself

This weekend, find a spot inside or outside your home where water can run or stand still: a tap, a trough, a wide basin filled to the brim. Sit next to it for five minutes. Listen for which noise falls back, and see what patch of sky the water mirrors. If a wall or a row of potted plants stands behind it, try putting the water near it and then farther away, and watch how the courtyard changes. Where does the courtyard feel calmest to you?
