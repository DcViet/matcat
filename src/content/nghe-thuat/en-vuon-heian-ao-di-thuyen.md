This example is mine, not a memory. You walk into a garden cafe with a fairly large lotus pond. From the veranda the pond looks like a photograph: the far bank, a row of trees, a few lotus flowers. Then the owner lends you a small rowboat. Out in the middle you glance back and see the veranda you were just sitting on, the roof, and its reflection in the water. It is the same pond, but now the scene keeps sliding past the bow, and the house has become part of the view.

More than a thousand years ago, Japanese aristocrats built gardens for exactly that moment in the boat.

## A Garden of Pond, Spring, and Boat

In *Japanese Gardens* (Taschen, 2003), Günter Nitschke divides the history of the Japanese garden into five archetypes. The first belongs to the Heian period (794–1185). Its Japanese name is *chisen shuyu teien*, word for word a "pond, spring, boating garden" (pp. 29–53). This was the first wave of Chinese influence in Japan. The gardens were very large, and according to Nitschke they looked more like a seascape than a mountain scene. The owners were aristocrats who designed their own gardens and used them as a setting for banquets.

This site already has two pieces on later Japanese gardens. In [Ryoan-ji: Three Ways to Read a Gravel Courtyard](/en/nghe-thuat/ryoan-ji-ba-cach-doc/), the viewer sits still on a veranda. In [Roji: The Path to the Tea House](/en/nghe-thuat/roji-loi-di-den-nha-tra/), you walk step by step on stones. The Heian garden came before both, and it chose a third way: it put the viewer in a boat.

One thing to know before reading on. Almost no Heian garden survives in its original form. What we know today was pieced together by later generations from picture scrolls, texts, and excavations. The picture at the top of this piece is a section of one such scroll, which I picked on Wikimedia Commons, not from the book: courtiers sit along a veranda, and on the pond below are two boats carrying musicians.

## The Shinden House Facing the Pond

This garden comes with a type of house, called *shinden*. I have redrawn its parts as a conceptual diagram:

![Conceptual plan of a shinden residence: the main hall in the middle, two side halls, two corridors running down to a fishing pavilion and a spring pavilion at the edge of the pond, a white sand court to the south, and the water path in red entering from the east and leaving to the southwest](/images/vuon-heian-ao-di-thuyen-en.svg)

The main hall sits in the middle and is symmetrical. From it, two corridors open toward two side halls. Right at the water's edge stand a fishing pavilion and a spring pavilion. In front of the house, to the south, is a court covered in white sand with nothing planted in it, a stage for ceremonies. The pond lies beyond that court.

So the whole house faces the pond. Sitting in the main hall, you get a straight-on view. Walk out to the fishing pavilion and you are already over the water. Step into a boat and you can look back at the house, like that glance over your shoulder at the cafe (the comparison is mine).

Nitschke adds two details. Toward the end of the Heian period the house grew less symmetrical, and its blocks were joined more freely. And *The Tale of Genji* describes a residence divided into four gardens for the four seasons, each one facing a different direction.

## Geomancy Decides Which Way the Water Runs

Heian builders did not dig a pond wherever they liked. They followed geomancy, learned from China. According to Nitschke, a good site is shaped like an armchair: a mountain for the back, two ranges of hills for the armrests, and the seat open to the south. Four divine animals guard the four directions, and each goes with something the site should have on that side:

| Direction | Animal | What should be there |
|---|---|---|
| East | Blue Dragon | a stream |
| West | White Tiger | a road |
| South | Red Phoenix | a marsh |
| North | Black Tortoise | a mountain |

The five elements were also tied to directions, colors, and seasons. The northeast was treated as the "demon gate." Kyoto has Mount Hiei shielding it on exactly that side.

The advice about water follows from all this. According to the *Sakutei-ki*, water should enter from the east, pass under the house, and leave to the southwest so that it carries bad influences away. Land inside the bend of a river is the "belly of the dragon," which is good land. Land on the outside of the bend is the dragon's back, which is bad.

## What the Sakutei-ki Says About Setting a Stone

The *Sakutei-ki*, meaning "a book on making gardens," is the oldest surviving text on the craft. It is thought to have been compiled in the second half of the 11th century, and its author may have been Tachibana no Toshitsuna, an aristocrat and a son of Fujiwara no Yorimichi. Neither point is certain. The book gathers rules that had been passed down by word of mouth, taboos included (pp. 55–60).

Nitschke cites a scholar named Tanaka, who sums the book up in four concepts. The plain glosses after each name are mine:

- ***Shotoku no sansui*:** the mountains and water of living nature. When you make a garden, look at real mountains and real water and work from them.
- ***Kohan ni shitagau*:** follow the "request" of the stone. Each stone has its own character, and the gardener has to grow quiet to hear how it wants to lie.
- ***Suchigaete*:** set things off-axis. Stones, islands, and the pond sit out of line with one another, unbalanced, right inside the symmetrical frame of the house.
- ***Fuzei*:** a "breeze of feeling," meaning the spirit of the place, and also the taste of the gardener or the owner.

The central act of the craft is called *ishi wo tateru*, "setting up stones," and people once used those same words for garden making as a whole. The book describes five kinds of pond shore, eight kinds of island, nine kinds of waterfall, then garden streams and ways of arranging stones.

The advice I like best is about imitation. If you want to remake a famous garden, you have to understand what its owner intended, then bring the features of that scene into your own garden so the two blend, without copying it outright.

The third concept made me think a little further. The shinden house is balanced, while the stones and islands in front of it are off-center on purpose. Nitschke subtitled his book "right angle and natural form," and he sees the beauty of the Japanese garden arising where those two layers overlap (pp. 8–12). The Heian garden is the earliest place we meet that pair.

## The Next Step: The Pond Becomes an Ocean, the Hall a Western Paradise

From the middle of the 11th century, the Fujiwara leaders built Pure Land temples with pond-and-island gardens. The form is still the Heian garden, but the meaning has changed: the pond is the ocean, and the hall of Amida Buddha is a model of the Western Paradise (pp. 46–48, 53–54). At Byōdō-in, built in 1052, the Phoenix Hall is mirrored in the pond. The Buddha faces east, and the worshipper faces west. At Mōtsū-ji, in Hiraizumi, the Heian-style rock shoreline is the best preserved, according to Nitschke. Most Kyoto temples of that time are gone, so much of this, too, is the reconstruction of later generations.

## Try It at a Body of Water Near You

Next time you sit beside some water, a lake in a park or a pond in the countryside, try two things. First, stand on the bank and fix in your mind what you see. Then, if there is a pedal boat or a bridge out to the middle, go out there and turn around to look at the spot where you were standing. See which view makes you want to stay longer, and how the house on the bank changes once it has a reflection in the water.
