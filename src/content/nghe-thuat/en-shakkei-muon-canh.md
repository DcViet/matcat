This example is mine, not a memory. You sit on the veranda of a narrow house, looking out at a courtyard of about ten square meters. The courtyard wall is taller than you are. But just above the top of the wall rises the crown of your neighbor's rain tree, and a little farther off, a patch of sky. You did not plant that tree and you do not water it. Yet every morning over your tea, the courtyard feels bigger because of it.

Japanese gardeners turned that trick into a technique with a name: *shakkei*, borrowed scenery.

## What Shakkei Is

In *Japanese Gardens* (Taschen, 2003), Günter Nitschke explains that *shakkei* literally means "capturing alive." It is the way of bringing far scenery into a garden design: a mountain range, a hill, a plain, a temple gate, a tower. Nitschke places it in the Edo period (1603–1868), when the older garden types had hardened into templates and people needed a new device (pp. 168–196).

The two characters 借景 that the Japanese use came from China. The garden treatise *Yuanye* (1635) gives its last chapter to "borrowing scenery," and sorts it into four kinds: distant views like mountains and lakes, nearby views like the neighboring buildings, views upward like clouds and stars, and views downward like rocks and ponds. So borrowing is hardly the private business of mountains. The neighbor's treetop in the example above counts as a "nearby" view.

## Four Planes, Receding

What makes shakkei work is that the borrowed view never stands alone. Nitschke divides the picture into four planes, receding from where you sit.

![The four planes of borrowed scenery: foreground, middle ground, frame, background](/images/shakkei-muon-canh-en.svg)

- **The foreground** plays only a small part. The eye slides across it.
- **The middle ground** holds objects that link the garden to the distant view: a stone lantern, a small island.
- **The frame** is trees, a hedge, the eaves. Clipped trees (*karikomi*) are mixed in with trees left natural, a hedge runs across, and together they cut the faraway scene into a picture with an edge.
- **The background** is the borrowed scenery itself.

I tend to think of it as three layers, near, middle, far, plus a frame wrapped around them. That phrasing is mine, not the book's. But it helps explain why the far view dissolves when you remove the frame: nothing is left to hold it together as one picture.

The biggest payoff is the sense of size. Nitschke describes a Kyoto garden of only about six hundred square meters that looks far larger than it is, thanks to clipped trees mixed with natural ones, a hedge, and a stone lantern placed on an island.

## Borrowing a Mountain Through the Trees at Entsu-ji

The example mentioned most often is Entsu-ji in Kyoto's Sakyo ward. The temple stands on the site of the Hataeda palace, a retreat of Retired Emperor Go-Mizunoo. Its dry garden lies east of the Buddha hall, dates from the early Edo period, and is now a national scenic spot. What makes it famous is Mount Hiei: the mountain does not stand outside the garden but is part of the composition. In Nitschke's account, trees in front frame it.

Another case is Ninna-ji, where a five-story pagoda is drawn into a frame between treetops. At Jiko-in, near Yamato Koriyama, Nitschke counts twelve devices for making a small site feel large, and shakkei is only the eleventh. Before it, you pass a narrow gate, a low dark tunnel, two bends, a few forks, and then take off your shoes and step onto matting. Almost half the site is given over to the approach. By the time shakkei arrives, the roof and eaves frame the dry garden and the Yamato mountains beyond. Like [the path to the tea house](/en/nghe-thuat/roji-loi-di-den-nha-tra/), it makes you walk slowly and through shade, so that when the view opens, it has force.

## The Price of Borrowing

Borrowed scenery does not belong to you. That is the weak point of shakkei, and Entsu-ji shows it clearly. According to Kyoto's tourism site, the view of Mount Hiei from the garden narrows year by year as buildings go up around the temple, and a condominium is reportedly about to be built directly in front of the garden.

The cover photo was taken at Entsu-ji, facing Mount Hiei: between the viewer and the mountain are a plaster wall, ordinary roofs, a few bare trees. The mountain is still there. But imagine a view where more than a row of trees stands between you and the mountain, and you can see how hard it is to keep a borrowed scene.

So anyone planning to borrow should first ask: will someone keep this for me? A mountain lasts. A neighbor's rain tree lasts until the owner decides to cut it down.

## The Whole Landscape Into the Room

The idea that a frame makes a landscape worth twice as much also turns up outside Japanese gardens. Le Corbusier wrote about it after his 1936 trip to Brazil, with the phrase "the whole landscape enters the room." Luis Barragán heard him in Paris in 1931 and repeated the idea in a 1951 lecture on the Pedregal. At the Salk plaza, two parallel, symmetrical blocks frame the Pacific, while the top is left open so sky and cloud can pour in. I wrote about [a patch of sky in a closed courtyard](/en/cong-trinh/barragan-mau-va-anh-sang/) in its own article.

At Lunuganga, Geoffrey Bawa's own house and garden, the line of sight to the south ends at a white shrine on a distant hill, and in the middle ground he placed a Chinese jar under a moonamal tree. The jar is exactly the "linking object" of the middle plane. The story of that place is in [Geoffrey Bawa: A Tropical House Is a Place to Pass Through and Stay](/en/cong-trinh/bawa-nha-nhiet-doi-di-xuyen-qua/).

Kengo Kuma goes the other way. He says architecture should be a frame for seeing the environment from the inside, and that a gardener should not stand outside admiring. He wants to give up the position of the outsider, though when he needs a frame he still uses one. There is more in [Kengo Kuma and the Art of Making Buildings Lighter](/en/cong-trinh/kengo-kuma-hat-hoa-vat-lieu-nhe/).

## Borrowing for Your Own House

There is a practical lesson here. Before drawing anything, walk around the site and see what the real view holds: a tower, a mountain range, a line of far trees, a patch of sky. Then use windows, the veranda, and the fence to take the frame. This suits narrow houses, which often have a single direction that looks out over a neighbor's big tree.

Two things to keep in mind. A beautiful view should not become a formula, because the frame depends on floor height, interior, even where you put the chair. And, as said above, a borrowed view can disappear.

If you like moving from scene to scene, [The English Landscape Garden: Walk a Loop and Pass Through a Series of Paintings](/en/nghe-thuat/vuon-phong-canh-anh/) is one option. For a garden seen from the water, there is [The Heian Garden: A Garden to See from a Boat](/en/nghe-thuat/vuon-heian-ao-di-thuyen/).

Tonight, try a short exercise. Sit in the spot where you sit most in your house, raise both hands to make a rectangular frame, and slide it around. Is there anywhere that holds just one treetop, one roof, one good patch of sky? If so, you have found your first borrowed scene.
