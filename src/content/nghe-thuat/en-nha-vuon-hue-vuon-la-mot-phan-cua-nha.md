Try a small exercise (this example is mine): take a sheet of paper, draw the outline of a plot, and ask "where does the house go?" In many cities you would draw a house covering almost the whole plot, with the leftover strip as a yard. In a Hue garden house the order is reversed. The plot runs from 1,000 to 15,000 square meters, and the house is only one corner of it. That range comes from the VnExpress article "Kiến trúc nhà vườn Huế" (2013).

So I want to read a garden house the way a guest would, walking in from the outside through the gate.

## Four kinds of owners

VnExpress sorts Hue gardens by who owns them: palace gardens, tomb gardens, temple gardens and family gardens. This post is about the last kind. According to the paper, about 2,800 garden houses remain in Huế, more than 1,000 of them over 200 years old, clustered in Phú Xuân, An Cựu, Vỹ Dạ, Tây Lộc and Thuận Thành. The numbers differ between sources: a Saigon Giải Phóng article, quoting the city's culture official, puts it at around 4,000 garden houses, 705 of them rường houses. I only use these figures to show the scale: thousands of gardens, not a handful of houses.

## From the outside in: plant fence, gate, screen wall

The edge of a garden house is not a wall. VnExpress says the garden is usually fenced with a trimmed row of tea hedge or hibiscus. A low hedge cannot hide a passerby, but it says clearly where this household's land begins.

Past the gate, the guest does not see the house right away. A study of the traditional Hue garden house (a summary posted in 2011) says the house usually faces south or southeast to catch the cool wind, and that in front of the main house stands a brick screen wall with a shallow basin beside it. The basin can be a dry pond, a small pond or a potted ornamental plant. The screen and basin split the path in two, so a visitor has to turn aside before reaching the yard and the porch.

A VietnamNet piece on the An Hiên garden house says the screen wall at the entrance is meant to drive away evil. Hue garden houses follow geomancy, and the study's author even compares the basin and screen to the Perfume River and Ngự Bình mountain of the Citadel. I cannot judge how far that holds. What you can see is plain: the screen blocks the straight view into the house and makes the visitor slow down and go around. In [Roji: The Path to the Tea House](/en/nghe-thuat/roji-loi-di-den-nha-tra/) I described a Japanese garden that also slows you down, stage by stage.

## Ornamental garden in front, fruit garden at the sides and back

In *Kiến trúc cảnh quan* (Hàn Tất Ngạn, Nxb Xây dựng, 1999, chapter 2, pp. 40-55), a Hue garden house has two kinds of garden with different jobs: an ornamental garden in front, with a small pond, a rockery and decorative plants, and a productive garden at the sides and behind. VnExpress describes it the same way and adds the fruit trees: longan, mangosteen, mango, thanh trà, orange, mandarin and hồng nhung, from both the northern and southern parts of Vietnam, plus a fish tank and a rockery.

![Conceptual plan of a Hue garden house: plant fence around the plot, gate on the south side, screen wall and basin, ornamental garden in front, rường house in the middle, side house beside it, fruit garden at the sides and behind](/images/nha-vuon-hue-vuon-la-mot-phan-cua-nha-en.svg)

The way I read this layout, each part of the garden has its own job. The part near the main house is for looking: someone sitting on the porch sees the pond, the rockery, a few shaped trees. The farther part is for harvesting. Both sit inside the same fence, and neither counts as "secondary."

## The rường house in the middle of the garden

The main house is a carved timber rường house used for ancestor worship, with a separate side house for the family's daily life. Home and shrine are kept apart, so two houses stand in the garden, each with its own work.

The study summary above lists three bays with two side bays as the most common form. The frame is made of columns, rafters and purlins joined with wooden mortises and pegs; columns stand on stone bases against damp, and the pitched roof is tiled. Because the frame is joined by mortise, parts can be taken apart and replaced. I add one thought, and it is my own: a timber house raised on stone with a low roof blends easily into the plants around it, rather than pressing down on the garden.

## The garden as a way of living

VnExpress notes that the garden here has everyday economic value and is also where the owners enjoy flowers, taste fruit and take their ease after the cares of the day. That one sentence captures the difference from the gardens we usually meet in history books.

Compare it with the [English landscape garden](/en/nghe-thuat/vuon-phong-canh-anh/), where every step passes a "painting" planned in advance. In a Hue garden house, beauty can also be edible: longan, mango and hồng nhung grow right in the family garden, trimmed enough to admire and still ready to pick when the season comes. People do not stroll through such a garden as if visiting a gallery. They live in it: they pick, prune, sit, make offerings, and notice now and then that the garden is beautiful.

Hàn Tất Ngạn also mentions, in his section on palace and temple landscapes, an old idea: where water gathers, good fortune gathers. A small pond in front of a house could therefore carry both a practical and a spiritual meaning, but the book says this about palace and temple landscapes, so I do not attribute it word for word to garden houses.

For floor plans, here is something you can apply to your own home today: do not let the garden be what is left after the rooms are placed. Try reversing the order. Draw the plants and the water first, then ask where the house should tuck in.

## Try it yourself

Tonight, try drawing the place where you live the garden-house way: a boundary, an entrance, a first place to pause, and then two colors of plants, the ones to look at and the ones to eat or use. If all you have is a balcony, that works too: an ornamental pot facing the seat, a few pots of herbs on the other side.
