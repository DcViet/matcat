Say you want to change the living room, and the builder asks how high the ceiling cornice should sit and how the curved door frame is made. If all you have is talk, each person pictures something different. One sheet with measurements and a few detail lines ends the argument much faster. That is my example, not a story from my own work, but it is how people have built houses for centuries.

More than two hundred years ago in Europe, drawings already did exactly this job. Their work was wider than most of us would guess.

## Drawings were never just for walls

The Metropolitan Museum of Art holds a large collection of Italian architectural and ornament drawings from the eighteenth century. In the 1975 catalogue edited by Mary L. Myers, the museum explains why these sheets sit with prints and photographs. The first curator in charge treated a printed architectural design as worth keeping as much as a painting. That choice shows how the museum saw the drawing: as one step on the road from an idea to a finished building.

The catalogue describes several kinds of drawing, and each had its own job:

| Type of drawing | What it was for | Example in the catalogue |
|---|---|---|
| Stage perspective | Setting up the space for a play or festival | The drawings of the Bibiena family |
| Temporary architecture | Built for one ceremony, then taken down | Vanvitelli's drawings for a royal wedding in Naples |
| Interior ornament | Shaping ceilings, walls and fireplaces | Drawings for private town houses in France |
| Print design | Carrying a model far away through books | Designs for Blondel's *L'architecture française* |

The stage drawings teach the most. Their makers built nothing. They drew a space that viewers would believe in for a few hours of a performance. To do that, they had to understand light, shadow and scale as clearly as a mason does.

## Drawing to win clients over

The drawing was also what the client held in hand. In eighteenth-century France, many interior drawings showed private town houses (hôtels particuliers), with ceilings, walls and fireplaces worked out in full. A wealthy client could see the room before the money was spent. A good presentation sheet persuaded far more than a list of costs.

The catalogue also includes "catalogue" sheets, which offered a set of ornament choices for a client to pick from, much like a pattern book. Such a sheet works as a model and as an invitation at once.

The story of Percier and Fontaine stays with me. They drew decorations for the coronation of Louis XVIII, but the ceremony never took place. The sheets survived; the ceremony did not. A drawing can outlast the event it was made for.

## Drawing for the craftsman

A drawing does not stop at the client. It moves from the first sketch to a finished sheet for building, and along the way it has to carry measurements.

Femke Speelberg and Furio Rinaldi wrote about a rare case in Metropolitan Museum Journal number 50 (2015). The drawing shows an altar with a tabernacle for the Pantheon in Rome, which was then the church of Santa Maria ad Martyres, commissioned by the Virtuosi. It has three elevations and a plan, measured in Florentine braccia. The handwriting on the sheet helped the authors confirm who drew it. The author turned out to be the sculptor Vincenzo de' Rossi, and the sheet shows him thinking like an architect: proportion, columns, a platform and a gable all appear on paper before they appear in a church.

The third stage matters most. A working drawing needs enough measurement that the builder does not have to guess. When the builder guesses, the building drifts off the drawing a little at a time.

## Why an old line still helps today

Someone might ask what hand drawings from the eighteenth century have to do with building a house now. I think the answer is more than it seems.

In [Drawing to See, Not to Show Off](/en/nghe-thuat/ve-de-thay/), I argued that a sketch helps the person drawing see something that does not exist yet. These old drawings do one more thing: they help other people see the same thing. A good presentation sheet lets a client feel safe spending money, and a good working drawing spares the builder from guessing.

In [Loos and Giving Up Ornament: A House Is Beautiful Because It Doesn't Show Off](/en/bai-viet/loos-ngoi-nha-khong-trang-tri/), I wrote about an architect who gave up ornament. Looking at eighteenth-century sheets shows that ornament once had a clear place. It told something to clients and craftsmen, and the person drawing knew what they were telling.

For someone about to build, the practical lesson is this. When you ask an architect for a sketch, ask what it is for: for you to choose, for the builder to follow, or to test an idea. A sketch that fits its purpose causes less argument than a beautiful one nobody knows how to use. You can also add three lines to your own brief: what the room is for, where the light comes from, and which parts you need to see with exact measurements.

Next time you pass a wall with a moulding, a curved door frame or a decorated staircase, try drawing it on a sheet of paper, using only the measurements you guess and a few words. When you finish, compare it with the real thing by eye, and you will see what you missed. That is the exercise the eighteenth-century draughtsmen did every day.
