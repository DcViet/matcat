Here is an example I made up, to make this concrete. You have a small front yard, about four meters on each side, and you have just bought a tree you like. You stand in the middle of the yard, unsure where it should go. One tree alone in the middle looks lonely. Three trees crowded together might feel cramped. It sounds like a small question, but professional gardeners ask the same thing: does this spot suit a single tree standing alone, or a group?

The two ways of planting do different jobs, and landscape books separate them quite clearly.

## A Single Tree on Its Own

In his textbook *Kien truc canh quan* (Landscape Architecture, 1999), Han Tat Ngan calls this the specimen tree: a tree with a handsome form, shape and color, in scale with the buildings, placed on its own so that its full beauty can show (chapter 3, pp. 154-159). He names three good places for one.

The first is an open space in a garden or a plaza. There the tree is the focus of the scene, so its shape must be distinctive and its color must differ from the mass around it. The second is beside a building, and here the tree's form depends on the house. A long house takes a tall tree with a pyramid crown, to make it look shorter. A tall house takes a low tree with a round crown, to bring it closer to people. Shrubs soften the hard corners at an entrance. The third is beside a path or a bend, where people look from very close, so a small flowering tree suits best: leaves, flowers and the line of the branches all have to hold up to a near look.

Han adds a point that is easy to forget: a specimen tree needs a backdrop. A good one is a blue sky, a wall, or a mass of trees behind it.

## A Group of Trees

A tree group is a few trees, woody or shrubby or both, gathered into one complete composition. Its job is quite different. According to Han, a group works as a bridge from scattered open space to a concentrated place, and as the "near view" for a stage. Beside a building it can steer the whole composition, mark an entrance, or ease the shift between forms: his example is a round group beside a theater, helping the dome pass gently into the garden. Beside water, choose weeping trees whose color differs from the trees on either side, so the reflection stands out. At the end of a road, a group closes the view and backs a statue.

I arranged the two ways of planting in a table so they are easy to compare:

| | A single tree | A group |
|---|---|---|
| Role | The lead, the focus of the scene | One mass that links, shifts, stresses or closes |
| What it asks of the tree | Shape, color, leaf and flower all worth looking at | Trees that suit each other, in the same direction and rhythm |
| The backdrop | Must contrast: sky, wall, mass of trees | Can serve as the backdrop for something else, like a statue or a spire |
| Best at | Open spaces, bends, beside the house | Water's edge, road's end, building corner, scene changes |

## A Tree That Frames a Grove

Look again at the cover, John Constable's painting of Salisbury Cathedral from the riverbank under a stormy sky. I read it with exactly these two ideas. On the left stands a large tree alone, leaning, against a patch of pale cloud. In the middle is a dense grove, and the white spire rises out of it. The tree on the left takes the lead. The grove in the middle serves as backdrop and closes the view for the spire.

Grant W. Reid, in *From Concept to Form in Landscape Design* (Vietnamese translation by Ha Nhat Tan, 2006), has two terms for this in chapter 3 (pp. 82-94): focalization, which gathers the viewer's eye to one place, and enframement, which uses something near to wrap around a distant scene. The way I have applied them to the painting is mine, not Reid's.

## Formal or Free

Choosing a tree or a group also depends on the layout of the whole garden. Han sorts the history of gardens into three tendencies. A formal layout organizes space geometrically, with elements mirrored across a system of axes, usually on flat ground, where trees naturally grow in balanced shapes or are clipped into even ones. On sloping ground, symmetry is hard, so it is often kept only along one main axis, as at the Villa Lante or the Villa Aldobrandini. A free layout is not symmetrical but is still balanced about the axis of the composition, with a network of paths, water, and thin woodland imitating nature, as in Chinese gardens, the English landscape park, and Japanese gardens (p. 78 and p. 100). The third kind mixes the two.

For trees it works like this. In a formal garden, trees or groups tend to come in pairs, set on either side of the axis, identical. In a free garden, one big tree on one side can be balanced by a smaller but denser group on the other. Reid calls the two kinds of balance formal and informal. I like to think of a pan balance: one heavy weight on one side, several light ones on the other, and the scale still level. For more on the English landscape garden, see [The English Landscape Garden: Walk a Loop and Pass Through a Series of Paintings](/en/nghe-thuat/vuon-phong-canh-anh/).

## Where to Stand to Look

Han makes a remark worth remembering about viewpoint, field of view and angle of view. Looking with the light behind you, you see detail clearly; looking into the light, you see only silhouettes. A thing changes its shape as you walk around it. And speed decides how much detail people can read: at speed the eye cannot keep up, so beside a highway you plant large, simple masses, while beside a footpath you may be intricate.

Applied to our question: a specimen tree suits the end of a route, where people stop and look closely. A group, as Han says, suits places that need to link, shift or close a view. If you want a tree to stand out, go stand where people will look at it, and see what is behind it.

## Choose the Species Last

A tree also has to survive. Han tells the story of the chò nâu trees planted on Hung Vuong Street in Hanoi: the species is used to multi-layered forest, so it grew poorly on the street and risked dying. He lists what to weigh: climate zone, soil, air humidity, light (a sun-loving tree does not belong in deep shade), and tolerance of polluted air. For street trees he advises a straight trunk, tough wood, a compact crown and deep roots so the sidewalk does not heave, with branches starting from three meters up (pp. 153-156).

That list was written for the 1990s. I add two points of my own: a tree that stands alone should withstand strong wind, and its branches should stay clear of power lines. Neither is in the book.

## Try It Yourself

Back to the four-meter yard. This afternoon, stand where people usually look at the yard from, maybe the gate or a window. Ask yourself three questions. Should this tree play the lead, or serve as a backdrop for something else? What is behind it? And if one tree feels lonely, would two smaller trees make it look better, or make the yard feel tight?
