This example is made up; it is not a memory. Picture a small office with a row of vents right at floor level, letting air in even when the windows are shut. In the rainy season, water blows in and soaks the legs of the desks. The first week, someone tapes a piece of cardboard over a vent. The week after, someone else swaps the cardboard for a board. Nobody means any harm; everyone just wants dry feet and a day's work done.

Something similar happened in a real building, on a much larger scale: the Mahaweli Building in Colombo, Sri Lanka. In my [article on Bawa](/en/cong-trinh/bawa-nha-nhiet-doi-di-xuyen-qua/), I wrote about how he loved houses that were little more than a roof and columns, with the air passing through. This one is about the only high-rise he ever built, twelve floors tall.

## A High-Rise Worked Out Line by Line

According to the paper by Tan Beng Kiang and David Robson (PLEA 2006), the building was originally the State Mortgage Bank and is now called the Mahaweli Building. Geoffrey Bawa designed it in 1972 and finished it in 1978, and it is the only high-rise he ever built. The site sits at an angle between Beira Lake and a park. The main principles of the design:

- **A form that catches wind and dodges sun.** The main faces point north and south, and the plan shape catches the northeast and southwest monsoon winds. The building is low with a broad base, because elevators were not yet reliable at the time. When the power goes out, people walk down a stairwell lit by daylight.
- **Air coming in at several heights.** Precast concrete vent blocks sit right at the sill, with a projecting slab above to keep the rain off, so air gets in even with the windows shut. Pivoting vertical windows and high openings between the offices and the stair lobby let air cross through.
- **Service cores pushed to the edge.** Stairs, elevators, and toilets sit on the perimeter with natural light and air. The two cores also act as a thermal buffer for the office block in the middle.
- **A shallow plan, with windows running column to column.** The windows run unbroken and bring light to the whole floor.
- **Shading from the building's own form.** Deep eaves and drop edges cut the low sun for most months of the year. Floors are polished cement, window frames are timber, and there are no false ceilings.

Ken Yeang calls the building a good example of a high-rise that responds to climate, but that is a qualitative judgment, with no measured performance behind it.

One small detail I like: the sloped surface and gutter on the projecting slab drain into a pipe behind a U-shaped column, and grooves on the facade keep the water from leaving dark streaks. Any tropical building has to deal with running water and mold.

## Then People Moved In

According to the same paper, right after the building was finished in 1978, its first occupants were foreign experts working for the Ministry of Mahaweli. They divided the open floor plans into smaller rooms and installed window air conditioners. The building was never used as intended, and it never had a post-occupancy evaluation. Bawa grew discouraged and left it out of his publications. It was not until 1998 that Robson and Daswatte published it for the first time.

Around 2005, the authors recorded the following:

- Dust and traffic noise made people shut their windows, especially on the lower floors. On the upper floors, papers blew around.
- The vent blocks at the sill were boarded over with plywood, because of rain blowing in and because moisture leaked out while the air conditioning ran.
- Glare was a problem, and heavy fabric curtains were pulled shut.
- Rooms were divided with tall partitions, turning the floors into a maze that blocks both light and air. This way of dividing the space also runs against the organization's hierarchical structure.

I want to stress the word "because" in those lines. Nobody boarded up a vent to wreck the design. Each person did the sensible thing from where they sat, and added together, the building turned into something else.

The data in the paper are observations and interviews, not measurements. The building has also been altered, so it is hard to treat as evidence in either direction: you cannot say the original design worked, and you cannot say it failed.

## Occupants Need a Reason to Open the Window

Bay and Ong’s *Tropical Sustainable Architecture* (2006) has a chapter showing this is not unique to Colombo. Ong and Cam use figures from Singapore’s public housing (chapter 6, pp. 87–97). Blocks lined up in parallel on a north-south axis, from the 1970s, work very well thermally, but they stand so close together that residents shut their windows for privacy. Later estates were staggered so windows do not look straight at each other, and face greenery instead. With a pleasant view, people open the windows, which brings in light and air and cuts electricity use.

The book says these figures are aggregated by flat type and do not prove cause and effect household by household, so they need careful reading. Even so, the rule they draw is worth keeping: instead of adding more technology, design "reasons to open the window," such as a view, some privacy, a tree. Putting the two stories side by side is my own reading; the two sources do not talk to each other.

## What the Authors Propose

The fixes they suggest hand the control of the wind back to the occupants:

- An outer noise barrier, and sliding panels to adjust airflow, like WOHA's "monsoon window" at One Moulmein Rise.
- Ceiling fans, and a mixed mode for the hot season.
- Perforated blinds to cut glare, and shading on the outside instead of curtains on the inside.
- An open plan with low partitions, plus a few closed rooms near the cores.

The lesson they land on sounds simple: natural ventilation has to be controllable, occupants need to be taught how it works, and a building needs to be evaluated once people live in it.

This is close to what I wrote in [Build a Tent or Light a Fire](/en/bai-viet/dung-leu-hay-dot-lua/) about two ways of conditioning an environment: a well-designed opening that nobody can open or close is just a hole.

## Taking It Home

A few things I think you can use right away, even in a very small house, and these are my own ideas. A vent should have a way to close when rain blows in, or sooner or later it will get blocked with something. Shading belongs outside the window, because an indoor curtain stops the sun but also stops the air and the light. And tall partitions easily turn crossing air into dead air, so I would choose low ones, or leave openings up high for the air to pass.

Try one thing. On a hot day, walk around your home or workplace and count how many windows and vents you have closed, blocked, or covered yourself. For each one, ask yourself: why did I close it? Dust, noise, rain, glare, or privacy? If that "because" can be fixed with a small screen, the window may get to open again.
