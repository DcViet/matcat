Your grandmother has an old wooden cabinet, dark, with doors worn smooth where so many hands have opened them. You need more storage, so you buy a new cabinet, and the whole family starts arguing about it. Buy one made to look old, with distressed paint and a curly handle, and the moment the two stand side by side the new one gives itself away as a fake. Buy a glossy one that looks nothing like it, and the old cabinet suddenly seems like something to haul out.

That example is mine, not a memory. But the problem in it, how to set the new beside the old so both can stand, is exactly Carlo Scarpa's problem.

Scarpa (1906–78) was an Italian architect. I file him under late modernism, with an interest in context. His way of working fits in one sentence: place contemporary architecture carefully inside an existing setting, using forms and materials that are recognizable but that add to what is already there, and respect the routes, the movement, and the views. The example *The Fundamentals of Architecture* (AVA, 2007) gives is Castelvecchio in Verona. The castle can still be read as a castle, yet it has become a museum with a contemporary sculpture garden.

The castle was built by the Scaligeri in the mid-14th century (1354 to 1356) and later became a military post under Napoleon. Scarpa turned it into a museum in several phases, roughly 1959 to 1973 (sources give slightly different dates). One famous detail is the equestrian statue of Cangrande I, set on a projecting concrete bracket where the castle's wings meet. This piece does not walk you through the rooms. It uses the castle as a place to ask a few questions. The cover is the outer curtain wall, the "old" that he had to set the new beside.

## Giving an Old Building a New Job

The AVA book defines adaptive reuse plainly: a vacant building is given a new function without destroying what makes it what it is. This is often more sustainable, because the materials and the form are kept. In the same entry, Castelvecchio stands next to Tate Modern, the power station that Herzog & de Meuron converted (1998–2000).

The entry mentions two other architects. At Santa Maria do Bouro (1989–97), Souto de Moura treated the ruin as more important than the name "monastery" and added new materials "among the things" already there. Kengo Kuma left a buffer between old and new, like a threshold in time. I wrote about both of them in [Souto de Moura: A Stone Wall Left as Stone](/en/cong-trinh/souto-de-moura-buc-tuong-da/) and [Kengo Kuma and the Art of Making Buildings Lighter](/en/cong-trinh/kengo-kuma-hat-hoa-vat-lieu-nhe/).

The AVA book also separates *space* from *place*. A place is somewhere with activity, events, and memory, and Castelvecchio is the book's example of what it calls *memory of place*. Here is how I understand it, and this part is my own idea: to keep a place is to keep its memory too, so whatever you add has to leave that memory readable.

## A New Layer That Speaks Softly

Another book says nearly the same thing from the side of exhibition design. In *Designing Exhibitions* (2006), Bertron, Schwarz, and Frey argue for placing a contemporary display layer over the rooms that already exist, so that the layer does not hide the valuable historic parts (pp. 20–21). Among the three respects they list, respect for the building means accepting the historic space and not overpowering it with added fixtures (pp. 22–23). These authors also call Scarpa an architect and an exhibition designer.

The book does not say Scarpa followed those two principles at Castelvecchio. I only set them side by side, in this arrangement:

| What has to be done | What the AVA book says of Scarpa | The exhibition book |
|---|---|---|
| Keep the old readable | The castle can still be read as a castle | Do not hide the valuable historic parts (pp. 20–21) |
| Give the new a manner | Recognizable forms and materials that complement | Do not overpower with added fixtures (pp. 22–23) |
| Think of the person passing through | Respect routes, movement, views | Respect the visitor (pp. 22–23) |

Read down each row and you hear the same question: how loudly should the new speak?

## The Edge Between the Two

Wherever old meets new, there is an edge. Here is how I read an edge: when you read a building, mark the edge with a thick line, then ask three questions. What is the edge made of (a step, a slope, a wall, or water)? How does it join the two sides (continuous, broken, or set back)? Where does a person stand to feel the edge? These three questions are mine, borrowed from the way of reading edges in Antje Steinmüller's lectures at the CCA (2010–2011).

![A conceptual diagram: a block for the old on the left, the new layer on the right, a thick red line between them for the edge, and three questions for reading the edge](/images/scarpa-castelvecchio-cai-moi-canh-cai-cu-en.svg)

One thing I have to say plainly. In those lectures, Scarpa appears in the group on edge manipulation and in the section on thresholds, but with a different building: Querini Stampalia in Venice (1961 to 1963), where canal water is let right into the entrance. So I will not claim to know how he handled the edge at Castelvecchio. I am borrowing the way of reading so you can try it yourself, as an exercise.

The AVA book defines a threshold as a recognizable boundary between inside and outside, or between two rooms: a stone step, a change of material, an eave, a door frame. Steinmüller's lectures add a concept, *liminality*, meaning the in-between state, open and still uncertain. By that reading, a threshold is a physical edge and also a way people use the place. I would add an idea of my own: between old and new there is such a threshold too, and whether the designer makes it thick or thin, sharp or soft is a decision that belongs to the designer.

## Drawing to See

Smith quotes Scarpa in *Architects' Drawings* (2005): he trusted only what he could see, and he saw an image only when he drew it. Smith also cites Merleau-Ponty, that a line does not imitate what has been seen but makes it appear (introduction, pp. 1–5). The exhibition book by Bertron and colleagues quotes the same line in its design section (pp. 34–70). Both books connect the exact detail of a building to hand sketching.

Among the six jobs a sketch can do in [Drawing to See, Not to Show Off](/en/nghe-thuat/ve-de-thay/), one is "evaluate," which means trying it for size. To me that fits the problem of setting new beside old: draw both on one sheet, see whether they sit well together, then adjust. That connection is mine; Smith does not write it. For a building you cannot test at full size, there is no cheaper test.

## Try It Once

Pick a spot near you where new meets old: a new iron gate beside an old whitewashed wall, a patch of tile set into an older floor, a new cabinet next to your grandmother's wooden one. Draw it in five minutes, and do not worry about looking good. Then go over the edge with the thickest line your pen can make, and answer the three questions in the diagram.

When you are done, try taking something away: if that edge were thinner, or thicker, would the two sides still stand comfortably together?
