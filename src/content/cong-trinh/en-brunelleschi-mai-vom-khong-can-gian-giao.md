Try building an arch out of a child's wooden blocks. The two sides go up easily. When you reach the block at the top, you have to hold it with your hand or slide a frame underneath, because until the keystone is in, the other blocks have nothing to lean on.

That example is mine, not a memory. Now blow the arch up to more than forty meters across, set it 55 meters in the air, and close the whole circle. What wooden frame could hold it? That was the problem Filippo Brunelleschi took on in Florence.

## A hole that waited a generation

The cathedral of Santa Maria del Fiore had a large gap left open right over the crossing. The octagonal drum, 13 meters high, was already finished in 1413. The dome that would cover it was another matter, because nobody knew how to build it.

Sources usually name three difficulties. The span was huge. Long, strong timber for a support frame was hard to find. And Gothic buttresses on the outside had been ruled out from the start. In 1418 the cathedral held a competition. According to an article in the magazine *Finestre sull'Arte*, eighteen architects took part with seventeen proposals, and the two names remembered best are Lorenzo Ghiberti and Brunelleschi. Brunelleschi was born in 1377, died in 1446, and trained as a sculptor before turning to architecture. On April 16, 1420, the two were appointed to run the work together, and construction began on August 7 of that year.

In *The Fundamentals of Architecture* (AVA, 2007), the Brunelleschi profile sums it up: an octagonal dome with two shells, wide enough to walk between them, chains around the base to hold back the sideways thrust, and built without scaffolding from the ground. The whole job took about 37,000 tons of material, including more than four million bricks.

## Two shells and the hidden ribs

The dome has two brick shells. The inner one is thick and is what you see from inside the cathedral. The outer one is much thinner, about 0.6 meters at the base and 0.3 meters near the top, and its job is to keep rain off the inner one. Between them is a gap, and in that gap a stairway climbs all the way to the lantern. Visitors today climb that same stairway.

The two shells do not stand apart. They are tied together by a skeleton: eight large ribs at the corners of the octagon, sixteen smaller ribs hidden between the faces, and several horizontal rings of brick. The heavy ribs at the corners are the white lines you can see running up to the top in the cover photo.

The curve of the dome is deliberate too. The Museo Galileo in Florence describes it as a pointed arch, the "quinto acuto," with a radius four-fifths of the base diameter. A pointed dome pushes less on its walls than a round one, so the base has less to carry.

## Herringbone bricks and chains around the base

The higher the dome rises, the more its surface leans inward. At that point a freshly laid brick, with its mortar still wet, wants to slide down. Brunelleschi had the masons lay the bricks in a herringbone pattern (*opus spicatum*), which he may have seen in Rome. According to Wikipedia, that pattern passes the weight of the courses being built to the nearest vertical ribs, so the dome holds itself up while it is still open.

To keep the dome from pushing the drum walls outward, he bound the base with rings made of sandstone and wood, joined by iron clamps. A chain works like the hoop on a barrel: the dome wants to spread, and the hoop holds it in. That is why the outside buttresses were not needed.

The talk about scaffolding needs care. Brunelleschi did not build a timber frame up from the ground, the way earlier domes were made. But the workers still needed somewhere to stand. According to the Florence museum's account, while the shell was nearly vertical they used scaffolds inside and then outside, and once it leaned too far he had a platform hung in the middle of the dome, resting on beams set into the lower levels. Some scholars even point out that an octagonal dome with flat faces should not, in theory, be able to stand by itself. So "no scaffolding" here means no forest of timber posts from the floor, not workers standing in thin air.

Millions of bricks and heavy stone also had to be lifted to that height. Brunelleschi built a hoist of his own for the job. Leonardo da Vinci later admired that machine.

I put this table together from figures in Wikipedia, the Museo Galileo, and the *Finestre sull'Arte* article. The sources differ by a few meters on the dome's height, so I rounded.

The AVA book says the span is about 42 meters, while the table says 45.5. They do not contradict each other. An octagon can be measured two ways: between opposite corners, or between opposite flat faces. When I multiply 45.5 by the cosine of 22.5 degrees, I get about 42 meters. That calculation is mine, not the book's.

In [The Pantheon: A Whole Room with One Round Hole Looking Up at the Sky](/en/cong-trinh/pantheon-anh-sang-tu-tren/), I wrote about the Roman room, 43.3 meters across. The dashed line below the figure is that width. The two domes are nearly the same size, but the Pantheon sits right on the ground, while the Florence dome had to be lifted 55 meters up.

## An architect who left no drawings

One detail made me stop. According to Wikipedia, Brunelleschi left no plans or diagrams explaining how he built the dome. That fits an idea in *Architects' Drawings* (Kendra Schank Smith, 2005, pp. 6–23). She writes that in the Renaissance, architects began to step out of the craftsman's role, take personal responsibility, coordinate many trades, and persuade whoever paid. The same book credits Brunelleschi with rediscovering perspective around 1420, the very year he took the job. I wrote about what drawings let us see in [Drawing to See, Not to Show Off](/en/nghe-thuat/ve-de-thay/).

I would guess, and this is only my own idea, that on a job lasting sixteen years the person in charge had to explain with words, models, and being on site every day, because drawings could not carry that load. The book does not say so.

He did not see it finished, either. The dome was consecrated on March 25, 1436. A second competition, for the lantern, was held later that year, and Brunelleschi beat Ghiberti again. Work on the lantern began in 1446, the year he died, and others finished it around 1461.

The idea of using shape to make a vault stand by itself goes further than Brunelleschi. In [Gaudi and the Hanging Chain: Turning a Model Upside Down to Find an Arch](/en/cong-trinh/gaudi-mo-hinh-day-xich/), I wrote about another man who found an arch by hanging a chain upside down.

## Try it yourself

Next time you see a photo of the Florence dome, count the white ribs running up to the top, and ask where the masons were standing up there. Or tonight, build an arch out of books, a few on each side and one at the top. You will see why every arch needs something holding it while it is still open.

In your own home, what is standing because of some hidden support you have never noticed?
