Picture the end of a workday. You step out of the elevator on the 12th floor and walk along the corridor. In front of your door there is a patch that is half indoors and half outdoors, with a chair and two potted plants. A few steps away, a neighbor sits in a similar spot outside her door and nods at you.

I made this scene up as an example. It is not a memory. But it comes close to what a Singapore architect set out to do more than forty years ago. Cheng Jian Fenn, of Design Link Architects, designed Bedok Court in 1982, and it was finished in 1985. It has 280 apartments in three blocks, from 4 to 20 stories tall, on a 3.4-hectare site in Bedok. He drew on Singapore's kampong villages, and his firm sums the idea up in four words: "landed living in the sky."

The cover photo shows a house in Kampong Lorong Buangkok, one of the kampong villages still standing in Singapore, with a wide roofed veranda opening onto the village lane. At Bedok Court, that veranda is lifted up to every floor.

## A village stacked up

In *Tropical Sustainable Architecture* (edited by Joo-Hwa Bay and Boon Lay Ong, 2006), chapter 5 spends about twenty pages on Bedok Court (pp. 59 to 80). According to the chapter, each apartment has a semi-open forecourt that connects to the shared corridor, and about 30 to 40 percent of each apartment's area is semi-open space. The site is truly dense: roughly 300 people per hectare, with a floor area ratio of 1.9. Even so, the architect wanted every home to have "a patch of green" of its own, the way a house on the ground has a plot of land.

The shared corridor here is called a "street in the sky." The three blocks each arrange it differently:

| | Layout | What the book records |
|---|---|---|
| Block 1 | One-sided corridor facing north | Best ventilation |
| Block 2 | A middle wing and two side wings | No standout feature is noted |
| Block 3 | A central corridor with staggered units | Best at limiting sun, worst ventilation |

I arranged this table myself to keep things short. As for neighbors meeting each other, the book finds all three about equal.

## The veranda is a buffer

![Conceptual diagram: two rows of boxes joined by arrows. The top row is a kampong house, going from village lane to veranda to room. The bottom row is a Bedok Court apartment, going from shared corridor to forecourt to room.](/images/bedok-court-lang-keo-len-cao-en.svg)

What I like about Bedok Court is how it leaves a layer of buffer. If your door opened straight onto the corridor, every step out would be a step into shared space. Here, between the corridor and the room there is a stretch that is half private and half shared. People sit there, read, talk, and children play.

The idea is not new. It comes from Jane Jacobs, whom Tzonis recalls in chapter 2 of the book: the social quality of a place is the quality of the moments when people touch each other's lives. Bedok Court's architect accepted a little less privacy in exchange for acquaintance. And that trade seems to have paid off. According to the survey, about 86 percent of residents named the forecourt their favorite space and used it at least weekly, about 90 percent did not feel a lack of privacy, and 66 percent knew their neighbors on the floors above and below. Residents also call the estate a kampong. They installed air conditioning, but they rarely turn it on.

If this sounds familiar, it may be because in [People Go Where People Are](/en/bai-viet/nguoi-den-noi-co-nguoi/) I wrote about how Jan Gehl looks at life on the street. The forecourt is a small street like that, only stood on end.

## How deep should a forecourt be? Two meters or more

A veranda that looks good on paper may not be one you can sit on. The survey team at Bedok Court found a threshold: a forecourt 2 m deep or more (plus a 1.4 m corridor) has enough room for sitting, reading, talking and children's play, and enough shade, with about 57 percent of the area covered. Narrower than that, you can only fit a few pots.

And shade matters more than wind. That surprised me most. In the book, Liang took measurements in block 1 in June 2004 at three places: inside an apartment, on the forecourt and out on the corridor. Midday solar radiation in the narrow corridor shot up compared with the wide, shaded forecourt, while the wind in the corridor was stronger. The result is that the feeling of heat in a semi-open space depends on the sun more than on the wind.

A few numbers to give you a picture (the book calls them rough guidelines):

- In a narrow corridor, keep radiation below about 700 W/m² and a breeze of 0.5 to 1 m/s still feels slightly warm, which is acceptable. If the breeze is under 1 m/s and the sun is strong, people find it very hot.
- On a wide forecourt, radiation below 100 W/m² with a breeze of 0.3 to 0.6 m/s is already comfortable, and leans cool when the wind picks up.

The book adds a short remark: in the tropics, slightly cool beats slightly warm. So the comfort rules for a room indoors do not predict how it feels in a semi-open space.

## Plants make the forecourt cooler

Plants help too. According to the book, a forecourt with many plants is about 0.4 to 2.5 degrees cooler than a bare one, depending on the floor and the season. The standout figure is about 2.3 degrees C at 12:30 on a July day on the 16th floor, with shading and wind about equal at both forecourts. And here is one more interesting thing: residents with more plants know more of their neighbors.

Maintenance deserves a word too. According to Bay, plants on the forecourt, tended by each household, are a way for greenery to sustain itself. The estate-wide measures, such as the leaf-area-based ratio Ong proposes in chapter 10, suit top-down management and need a body that looks after upkeep.

## Two cautions

First, Bedok Court is a gated estate. The "street in the sky" there is semi-public: only residents and their guests walk in. Most Singaporeans, about 84 percent, live in public housing built by the state (HDB), and they are quite different from this estate's residents. And the figures come from a survey of one building, so don't turn them into law.

Second, Tzonis warns in the same book: don't put everything on the environment. Designers only create conditions. Meeting does not always mean agreeing, and it can even breed conflict.

One detail stuck with me most. From 1986, planning in Singapore switched to counting total floor area, so housing estates built afterwards rarely have wide forecourts. In other words, whether a forecourt survives depends on how people count square meters. This is my own reading: when the floor-area rule does not count the semi-open part, or counts it like living space, the forecourt is probably the first thing to be cut.

## Measure your own forecourt

If you live in a townhouse or an apartment, try measuring your balcony or the corridor outside your door. How many meters deep is it? Does it fit one chair and a person sitting in it? At noon, is there shade there or only sun?

Maybe a few dozen centimeters more is all it takes for that spot to become a place to step out and sit, and for neighbors to learn each other's faces.
