Picture an ancestral house in the countryside. The grandfather pours the foundation and raises the middle bay, then dies with the roof half done. His son carries on, but wants to drop the wide porch the old man left, to save money. By the grandchildren's time the family is large and needs another bay. In the end the house still stands, still one house, though the three of them never sat down together to draw it.

I made this example up; it is not a memory. But it is a small version of what happened in the Vatican, where St. Peter's took more than a hundred and twenty years to finish.

## One Foundation Stone, Over a Hundred Years of Building

There was an older church on the site first. The Metropolitan Museum of Art Bulletin (Winter 1982–83), issued with the exhibition *The Vatican Collections*, says Emperor Constantine had it built around 320 on the tomb of Saint Peter. It had twenty-two columns to a row and, according to the issue, was the first church with a cross-shaped plan. It stood for over a thousand years, long enough for Charlemagne to be crowned there in 800.

In 1447 Pope Nicholas V wanted to rebuild the whole of Rome around the church, and asked Alberti to draw up the plan. The work stopped when the pope died. According to that same issue, this would happen again and again.

Then came Julius II, who wanted to "renew" the Rome of the emperors. On 18 April 1506 the first of four giant piers was laid. On 18 November 1626 Pope Urban VIII consecrated the church. Between those two dates, the issue says, about twenty architects took turns, and four of them left a decisive mark: Bramante, Michelangelo, Maderno, Bernini.

## Who Left What

I put this table together myself.

| Person | What they did | What remains |
|---|---|---|
| Bramante (from 1506, died 1514) | Greek cross, a great dome, eight smaller domes | The scale and overall plan |
| Sangallo and a few others (1514–1546) | A wooden model that took seven years, an outer ambulatory | Work almost at a standstill, including the sack of Rome in 1527 |
| Michelangelo (from 1547, at age 71) | Tore down the outer ambulatory, gathered it into one mass | Giant Corinthian columns on the outer walls, the drum of the dome |
| della Porta and Fontana (1588–90) | Raised the dome | A dome taller than a hemisphere |
| Maderno (about 1607–14) | Added a nave, built the facade | The Latin-cross plan, the facade of 1612 |
| Bernini (from the reign of Urban VIII) | The bronze canopy, the Chair of Saint Peter, the piazza | Colonnades like open arms |

Each row is a decision that the next person had to live with. Bramante drew the church as a mass pulled toward the middle: cross, square and circle fused into one. In *Experiencing Architecture* (1959), Rasmussen uses that very plan as an example of architecture of cavities: blacken the walls and you see round spaces and half-round niches that look hollowed out of one great block of stone. In *Architects' Drawings* (2005), Kendra Schank Smith quotes Ackerman saying that earlier architects only drew buildings, while Bramante *sculpted* them.

Michelangelo took over in 1547, already 71. He found Sangallo's model dark, full of nooks, and tore down the outer ambulatory. In Vasari's words, quoted in the Bulletin, he made it "smaller but grander." He died in 1564, and the dome was raised by others afterward, in less than two years (1588–90), with more than six hundred workers going almost without rest. The dome is 138 feet across, about 42 meters. Giacomo della Porta made it taller than a hemisphere, and that upward reach later became a mark of the Baroque.

## A Hundred-Year Argument

The biggest tug-of-war was over the plan. Bramante and Michelangelo wanted a perfect shape, symmetrical around one center. Alberti also thought a church like that was more ideal than a Latin cross. But in 1605 Pope Paul V had the last of the old church pulled down, opened a competition for the facade, and decided to add a nave. The needs of the liturgy and of a crowd won out over pure geometry.

The price, according to the Bulletin, was that Maderno's seven-bay facade looks "too wide for its height," because its two outer bays were meant as bases for bell towers that were never finished. In *Vers une architecture* (1923), Le Corbusier regrets that the popes lengthened the nave and spoiled the view toward the dome. Rasmussen, for his part, remembers what it feels like for a pilgrim inside: giant columns running up through several stories take away the sense of scale, like Gulliver among the giants.

Each person kept altering the last, and yet no one threw out the old frame entirely. Venturi, in *Complexity and Contradiction in Architecture* (1966), even looks straight at the untidy spots: on the back of the church, Michelangelo set false windows beside column capitals larger than the windows themselves. I wrote about a way of thinking close to his in [Both, Not Just One](/en/bai-viet/venturi-ca-hai-va/).

## Bernini and the Open Arms

Last comes Bernini. Under Urban VIII he made the gilded bronze canopy (the baldacchino), about 28 meters tall, with twisting columns modeled on the twisting columns of the old church, so that the old kept a trace inside the new. He also made the Chair of Saint Peter (1656–66), in which light from a window becomes part of the work.

The best known is the piazza. After 1656 he drew semicircular colonnades around an ellipse with an obelisk in the middle: four rows of columns deep, three passages, the middle one for carriages and the two side ones for people. The columns are laid out like the spokes of a wheel, so standing at the exact center of the ellipse you see only one row. People compare it to the arms of the Church. According to the book, the colonnades also make Maderno's facade look taller, and I suspect that was one way of answering the "too wide" problem. As for the obelisk, Domenico Fontana raised it in 1586 with 907 men and 75 horses.

## The Frame That Holds Everything

So what let so many hands make one thing? I think it was the frame Bramante set at the start: the scale, the center, the idea of one perfect shape. Later people did not drop it, but they were allowed to argue with it. Michelangelo gathered it in, Maderno stretched it out, Bernini reached beyond it. This is my own reading; the books do not put it quite this way.

Records played a part too: Fontana's book, the engravings, the wooden models let later people know what earlier ones had thought. But records can also mislead. Dupérac's engraving of 1569 was once taken as Michelangelo's original, when it is not reliable about his intentions.

## Bringing It Home

I think Vietnamese houses often grow in just this way: a foundation poured for two floors, then a third added when the children grow up. So when you draw the first part, think about the later parts: how much the foundation can carry, whether the stairs have room to connect. That is my own idea, not something the Vatican teaches.

Try one thing: take a sheet of paper and draw the house you live in, then write beside each part which generation it belongs to. Do you see any part where someone before you thought ahead for someone after? And if you are the one who adds the next bay, what frame will you leave for the person who comes after you?
