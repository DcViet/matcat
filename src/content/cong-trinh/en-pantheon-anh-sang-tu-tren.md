Row houses in Vietnam often have a light well in the middle. Close to noon, the sun slips through the glass in the roof, prints a patch of light on the stairwell wall, and then creeps along bit by bit... The neighbors' houses stand on both sides, so there is no window to open, and still, thanks to that patch, someone sitting all the way down on the ground floor knows whether it is sunny outside or clouding over.

I made this example up; it is not a memory. I bring it up because there is a room in Rome that does the same thing, only much bigger and all the way through: the Pantheon. The wall is closed all the way around, and the whole round room has a single round hole at the top of the dome, looking straight up at the sky.

*The Fundamentals of Architecture* (AVA, 2007) places the Pantheon in the Roman period, around 118–126. The temple you see today is a rebuilding under the emperor Hadrian, somewhere between about 118 and 128. Who designed it is not certain, and I come back to that in the section on the dome.

## Coming in from the Street

In chapter VIII of *Experiencing Architecture* (1959), the chapter on daylight, Steen Eiler Rasmussen describes the way in. You walk through a tangle of streets, step up into the columned portico, then enter the great round room and meet a gentle light pouring down from high above.

He points out one thing about the shape: the cylindrical wall is exactly as tall as the radius of the dome. Continue the dome downward and you get a complete sphere that fits inside the room. The round opening at the top is the only connection to the outside world. Through it, a person standing in the temple sees the sky, not the streets.

## One Source of Light, and Real Shadows

Rasmussen puts the Pantheon among rooms that are closed on every side and open only at the roof. The light comes from a single source, so it has a clear direction, and shadows look like shadows. In [Good Light Is Not Plenty of Light](/en/bai-viet/anh-sang-tot-khong-phai-anh-sang-nhieu/) I retold this idea of his: a room lit evenly from all sides flattens its forms, while light with a direction is what makes a volume stand out.

When the sun comes in at a slant, the light falls through the opening as a cylinder-shaped beam. The floor is paved in stone squares and circles and throws light back into the corners, so according to Rasmussen no part of the room is black.

The painting on the cover, which Giovanni Paolo Panini made around 1734, shows nearly all of this: the round hole with blue sky and clouds, a patch of sun on the wall at right, the floor of squares alternating with circles. The books do not mention this painting; I chose it because it matches the description.

Rasmussen also observes that buildings copying the Pantheon lose that balance as soon as they change the proportions or cut extra openings. His example is Copenhagen Cathedral: the vault there has three openings, and what you get is one long streak of light that gathers nowhere, which in his view leaves the interior bland.

## Kahn: A World Within a World

Louis Kahn rarely talked about other people's buildings. Robert Twombly, who edited *Louis Kahn: Essential Texts* (2003), notes that the Pantheon is the place he came back to most. Kahn called architecture "a world within a world": a building sets up a place of its own for one human activity, different from what is outside. The Pantheon is the example he reached for most often.

In his talks (Twombly, pp. 151, 220, 252), Kahn read the room like this:

- It is round and not square, because corners would point in a direction and immediately suggest a place for an altar.
- The light comes only from the round opening, so strong that it "cuts," and people avoid standing right under it.
- The entrance door is the one spot that is not pure.
- The room faces no single direction of worship, so it suits every religion. He thought it expressed an "inspired ritual," as opposed to a ritual that is dictated.

Kahn also said that the emperor Hadrian may have intended a room 300 feet across, but no one could build it, so he pulled back. Kahn called that "economy," meaning whether someone is able to make the thing, not how much money there is. This is Kahn's telling. The room's real width is only about 43 meters, and I do not know of a historical source that confirms the 300-foot plan, so please do not treat it as verified.

On one point the two men seem to say opposite things: Rasmussen finds the light gentle, and Kahn finds that it "cuts." My guess, and it is only mine, is that one of them is looking at the whole room while the other is talking about the exact spot where the beam lands.

| Reader | What he looks at | What he sees in the Pantheon |
|---|---|---|
| Rasmussen | Light and proportion | One directional source, real shadows, a sphere that just fits |
| Kahn | What the room is for | A world of its own, facing no direction of worship |
| Le Corbusier | Form and idea | Few words, one idea, bold, unified |

I arranged this table myself. The last row comes from the chapter "The Lesson of Rome" in *Towards a New Architecture* (1923, pp. 149–173), where Le Corbusier praises the Pantheon, the Colosseum, and the Roman aqueducts in roughly those terms. He also complains that the Romans built a superb chassis and poor bodywork, meaning the fussy decoration.

## The Dome

The temple standing today is not the first one. Agrippa built a temple here in the late first century BC, but it burned in 80 AD, was rebuilt under Domitian, and burned again in 110. What you see now is Hadrian's version, usually dated to about 126. The inscription on the front still carries Agrippa's name, because Hadrian had the old wording cut again. That is also why the start date is hard to pin down: working from the stamps on the bricks, the scholar Lise Hetland argues that construction began around 114, under Trajan.

The designer is not recorded with certainty either, since no inscription names anyone. People often mention Apollodorus of Damascus, Trajan's architect. That guess rests on style, not on direct evidence, so it is best taken as a reasoned conjecture.

The measurements are much clearer. The room is about 43.3 meters across, or 150 Roman feet, and it is about as tall from the floor to the opening, so the sphere Rasmussen describes can actually be measured. The opening is about 8 to 9 meters wide, depending on how it is measured. The drum wall is about 6.4 meters thick. The dome is about as thick at its base and thins to roughly 1.2 meters around the opening.

It is cast in Roman concrete, and the clever part is that the concrete changes from bottom to top. The thickest part uses travertine, then crushed brick and tile, and higher up tufa and light pumice. The higher the dome goes, the lighter it gets, so it pushes less sideways on the wall. The inside of the dome is divided into five rings of coffers, 28 in each ring, 140 in all, and the coffers and the opening make the dome lighter still. The dome's weight is carried down into eight piers set inside the wall.

Francis Ching's dictionary has a detail that set me thinking: a Greek temple was for worship inside, and yet its builders cared most about how it looked from outside. The Pantheon, as I read it, is the reverse. The most memorable thing about it is a room.

At the [Church of the Light](/en/cong-trinh/nha-tho-anh-sang-ando/), Ando cut a slit in a wall. At [Kimbell](/en/cong-trinh/kimbell-kahn/), Kahn himself opened a slit at the top of the vault and then made the sun take a detour over a reflector. The Pantheon came long before both and does it more simply than either: one round hole, left open, right at the top.

Nobody builds a dome at home. But a light well, a double-height opening, or a pane of glass over the stairs is something many houses have. When you have a free moment, stand there twice, once near noon and once in the late afternoon. Watch where the patch of sun lands, which way the handrail's shadow falls, and which corners stay bright because the walls and floor throw light back.

Then ask yourself: if that room lost one of its side windows, what would you lose, and what would you start to see?
