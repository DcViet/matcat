Say you are walking along the coastal road in northern Portugal, phone in hand, searching for "famous swimming pool." The map tells you that you have arrived. You look around: a low concrete wall, a few rocks, the sea. No gate, no roof sticking up. You have to take a few steps and look down before you see the blue water lying below.

This example is mine, not a memory. But it is close to what people say about the tidal pools at Leça da Palmeira, a work by the architect Álvaro Siza: the first things you notice are earth, rock and sea, and the architecture comes second.

## A 26-year-old and a hollow by the shore

Leça da Palmeira is a seaside town in Matosinhos, right next to Porto. According to the documents on the project, in 1959 the town council approached a firm specializing in maritime works to build a bathing place on Leça beach. Siza was born in 1933, so he was 26 when he was brought in. Construction began in 1961 and the pools opened in 1966.

What I like is that the first decision was not about shape. The site chosen was a natural hollow beside the shore, which saved a lot of excavation. Before drawing anything, he had let the land decide part of the job.

The beach there is jagged. The photo at the top shows it well: dark brown outcrops rise out of the sand, and the edge of the pool runs right up to their feet. Look closely: in many places where the pool meets the rock, the concrete wall is only a low ledge, as if the outcrop had already done the wall's work.

## Going down like entering an alley

The complex runs parallel to Avenida da Liberdade, between the coastal road and the Atlantic, and is nearly out of sight from the road. From photographs of the entrance, you go down a ramp from the seafront promenade, then slip between concrete walls with a cantilevered cover, then along a shoreside passage with a timber roof. Siza describes the small buildings here as walls of raw concrete under a timber roof clad in copper sheet.

I read the route in stages:

| Stage | What you see | What the architecture does |
|---|---|---|
| The street | A low wall, almost nothing | Hides behind the road surface |
| The ramp | Raw concrete, cantilevered cover | Lowers your view, shuts out the street |
| The covered path | Timber, shade, the sound of waves | Prepares you for stepping outside |
| The pool | Water among rocks | Lets water and stone take over the view |

I arranged this table myself; it is not from a book. It only shows that the route is most of the "design" in this building. The water was already there; architecture decides in what order you reach it.

## Following the land means three concessions

Antje Steinmüller's lectures on architectural analysis (CCA, 2010-2011) contain two groups of devices that bear directly on this. The "ground manipulations" group puts Siza beside Asplund and Snøhetta, architects who bend the ground instead of setting a block on top of it. The "edge manipulations" group places the Leça pools next to Scarpa's Querini Stampalia and White's Seabath in Kastrup: three works that operate at the water's edge. For more on Scarpa, see [Scarpa at Castelvecchio: Setting the New Beside the Old So They Sit Well Together](/en/cong-trinh/scarpa-castelvecchio-cai-moi-canh-cai-cu/).

At Leça I see three concessions, which is my own way of putting it:

- **Conceding height.** The building sits below the road and does not stand in front of the sea.
- **Conceding shape.** The pool's edge and the walls follow the outcrops instead of forcing the rock into straight lines.
- **Conceding material.** The concrete stays raw and does only what the rock cannot: joining, covering, holding back.

The architect Alves Costa called the result "an artificial world within the natural landscape." Here is how I understand that: it is artificial because there are walls, steps and water treated to meet hygiene rules; it is natural because it is hard to say where the rock stops.

In *Architects' Drawings* (2005), Kendra Schank Smith mentions a detail that points the same way in her discussion of Siza's sketches. Portuguese has two words, *olhar* for looking and *ver* for seeing and understanding; for Siza, the architect's tool is the ability to "see." On a rocky beach like Leça, "seeing" means knowing which rock is already enough to be a wall.

## A building kept by repairing little

The pools were classified as a national monument in 2011, and in 2017 they were added to Portugal's tentative list for UNESCO recognition, together with the Boa Nova tea house, also by Siza, about a kilometer away. They are also the only Portuguese building in the book *100 Buildings: 1900-2000*, edited by Thom Mayne.

The building has had its ordinary troubles too. About 500 square meters of copper cladding and piping were stolen, and the city spent 150,000 euros on repairs in 2004. From 2019 to 2021 a major restoration cost 1.3 million euros and touched the reinforced concrete structure, the main tank and the seawater filtration system. Siza took part and adjusted the northern section, where a restaurant had once been planned but never built. The interior timber boarding was redone with wood reclaimed from demolished buildings. The pools are open only from June to September.

The restoration record says that nearly half a century of poor maintenance cracked the concrete walls, though Siza says the cracks are not a structural worry. A layer of burnt oil about half a centimeter thick, left by earlier treatments of the wood, had to be removed without damaging the grooves the years had worn into it, and that was the most time-consuming job. I take a small lesson from this: to keep a living building by the sea, you cannot add anything on top. You can only repair exactly what has failed.

## Try this one afternoon

Your house or the lane you walk every day has its own "outcrops": a tree trunk, the slope of the yard, a neighbor's old wall. This afternoon, stand at a spot like that and ask: if I had to add one thing, what would I let it give way to? Height, shape, or material? You do not need to draw anything. Just pick one thing to concede, and see how the place changes in your eyes.

If you like reading someone who worked beside Siza, the piece on [Souto de Moura: A Stone Wall Left as Stone](/en/cong-trinh/souto-de-moura-buc-tuong-da/) shows a man who once worked in his office going another way with stone: keeping it raw right in the wall.
