Lay a sheet of A4 paper across two stacks of books. Put a cup of water in the middle and the paper sags. Now fold the sheet into a trough, set it back down, and put the cup on again. The cup sits still, and the paper needs nothing to hold it up.

This example is mine, not anyone's memory. But it is close to what Oscar Niemeyer (1907–2012) found when he worked with concrete: a thin sheet that knows how to curve can stand by itself. The curve people take for his playful signature was sometimes simply a way to build.

## What he said about the right angle

In his memoirs Niemeyer wrote that he was drawn to free, sensual curves. The line is quoted so often that it sounds like a slogan. I read it as a statement of choice: he picked the curve, nobody pushed him into it.

He did not start out a stranger to the right angle. By his own account, his architecture was strongly shaped by Le Corbusier. He worked close beside him on the Ministry of Education and Health in Rio, and later contributed to the design of the United Nations headquarters. I told a little of Le Corbusier's story in [A House Is a Machine for Living In: The Question Before the Shape](/en/bai-viet/le-corbusier-huong-toi-mot-kien-truc-moi/). Niemeyer learned from it, then went another way.

## Pampulha: wall and roof as one

In 1940 Juscelino Kubitschek, then mayor of Belo Horizonte, asked Niemeyer to design a group of buildings for the new suburb of Pampulha. The complex was finished in 1943, and its best-known piece is the Church of Saint Francis of Assisi.

The church uses a parabolic vault of reinforced concrete that is both structure and enclosure. Put plainly, there is no wall with a roof laid on top. There is one curved sheet that rises from the ground and folds over. The folded paper from the opening is the same idea in miniature.

![Concept diagram comparing a square frame made of roof, beam and columns with a curved shell where roof and wall are one continuous sheet](/images/niemeyer-duong-cong-cua-brasilia-en.svg)

The structural engineer Joaquim Cardozo is recorded as calling the merging of wall and roof into a single element at this church revolutionary. The diagram above is mine, drawn to make the idea easy to see. It is not a drawing of the church.

Inside, Candido Portinari painted fourteen Stations of the Cross. Outside, Roberto Burle Marx designed the landscape. One odd but true episode: for fourteen years church authorities, led by Archbishop Antonio dos Santos Cabral, forbade Catholic services here. Time reported that he called it "the devil's bomb shelter." The church was consecrated only in April 1959. In 2016 it was listed by UNESCO with the rest of the Pampulha ensemble.

## Brasília: sixteen columns and a half-buried nave

In September 1956, soon after taking office, President Kubitschek asked Niemeyer to help build a new capital. The master plan belonged to Lúcio Costa, who won the competition. The main buildings belonged to Niemeyer and were finished by 1960, among them the National Congress and the Alvorada Palace. In *The Fundamentals of Architecture* (AVA, 2007), Niemeyer appears with Brasília in 1958 to 1960, and the book observes that repeated elements give the whole city a unity of form.

The cathedral of Brasília is where his curve goes furthest. Its foundation stone was laid in September 1958. By April 1960 the structure was done, with only the roof visible above ground. It was consecrated in 1968 while still unglazed, and officially opened on May 31, 1970. The structure is sixteen identical concrete columns, each weighing ninety tonnes, arranged as a hyperboloid. The columns spread from a small ring at the base, then curve and flare wide at the top, so from far away they look like a hand opened toward the sky.

Most of the church lies below ground. You walk down first, then enter the nave, and when you look up the sky shows through glass. The stained glass is by Marianne Peretti, made in 1990, covering about 2,000 square meters. Three angel sculptures hang on steel cables above the nave, the largest 4.25 meters long and 300 kilograms.

## The price of giving up the right angle

Leaving the right angle is not free. In a beam-and-column frame each part does one job and every builder understands it. With a curved shell, a miscalculation in one place unbalances the whole sheet. You need an engineer who can compute it, formwork that can be built in a curve, and a site with a lot of patience. The Brasília cathedral took nearly twelve years from foundation to opening. I will not claim the curve caused all of that, but it shows the work was never quick.

Still, builders have known curves for a long time. Look at [Brunelleschi's Florence dome](/en/cong-trinh/brunelleschi-mai-vom-khong-can-gian-giao/), or [Gaudi's hanging chain](/en/cong-trinh/gaudi-mo-hinh-day-xich/): there too the curve comes from how the material carries load. Niemeyer's difference is that he did it in reinforced concrete, which can be poured into any mold, and he added a rhythm he called sensual. I read that as the poetry in his work, not the calculation.

One more thing the biographies do not skip: after the military coup of 1964, Niemeyer left Brazil, opened an office in Paris, and returned only in 1985, when the dictatorship ended. He kept drawing those curves abroad, so the curve traveled with him.

## Taking it home

I do not think the shape is what is worth bringing home. A concrete shell roof in tropical rain will be hot and hard to drain, and I have no figures to say whether it suits. What is worth bringing home is the question: in my house, which two parts are doing two jobs that could be one?

Try a small one: fold the paper from the start into a trough, set it across two stacks of books, and add heavier things bit by bit. How much does it carry, and which fold made it stiff?
