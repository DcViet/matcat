This example is mine, not a memory. Say you rent a single room. By day you fold a screen against the wall, and the room becomes a roomy workspace. At night you pull the screen out and half the room turns into a sleeping corner. One screen, and the room has two personalities.

In 1924, in Utrecht, a Dutch architect did the same thing to an entire floor of a house, with wooden panels running on tracks. The house carries the names of the woman who lived in it and the man who drew it: the Rietveld-Schröder House.

## A Cabinetmaker and a Widow with Three Children

Gerrit Rietveld (1888–1964) trained as a furniture maker before he became an architect, and the house shows that origin at once. The client was Truus Schröder-Schräder, recently widowed and raising three children. She wanted a house with as few walls as possible, and an inside that connected to the outside. According to Wikipedia, Rietveld sketched a first scheme and she turned it down. After that the two of them sat side by side and worked out the final one.

The house stands at Prins Hendriklaan 50, at the end of a row of attached brick houses, sharing one wall with the last of them. When it was built, open polder land lay in front of it at the edge of the city. Schröder lived there from 1925 until she died in 1985. Rietveld also lived in it in his last years. Today it is a museum run by the Centraal Museum together with a foundation, and UNESCO added it to the World Heritage List in 2000.

## The Upper Floor: Walls That Slide

The ground floor is fairly familiar. A central staircase connects the kitchen to a few sitting rooms and bedrooms. The surprise is upstairs.

On paper, the second floor is listed as an attic because of the fire rules of the day. In practice it is one large room, and only the toilet and bathroom have real walls. The rest is divided by sliding and swiveling panels. Push them aside and the floor becomes a single space. Pull them out and in the evening it splits into several rooms. The house's own site says two bedrooms and a living room with a table and a stove. Other sources count three bedrooms. The number depends on how the panels are set, so I won't side with either.

I redrew the idea above to make it easier to see. It is a conceptual diagram, not to scale, because I could not find reliable measurements of the upper floor. The number of rooms and the wall positions are only illustrative.

One small detail I like: according to Wikipedia, one panel has a round hole cut out so that when it slides sideways it can pass a cupboard and a light switch. The designer thought about every light switch.

The corner window upstairs follows the same idea. When it opens, the frames swing out flat against the wall and the corner nearly disappears, so someone sitting inside feels outdoors. The staircase can be closed off too: a sliding panel covers it when needed.

## Color and Straight Lines

Look at the house from outside and you see at once that it is not a solid block. It looks like many flat planes and thin bars set beside each other, as if sliding past one another. That is how it manages so many projecting balconies. The wall planes are painted white and several grays, the window frames are black, and the slim bars carry the primary colors: red, yellow, blue. The yellow post in the cover photo is one example.

This approach is called De Stijl, the Dutch art movement whose best-known figure was Theo van Doesburg. According to *The Fundamentals of Architecture* (AVA, 2007), the movement wanted to join artistic ideas to real space and find harmony by reducing form and color: run only horizontal and vertical, use primary colors with black and white, let planes cross each other, and make interiors that can be taken apart. The Schröder House is the book's best-known example of this line.

Rietveld had already worked this way on a much smaller object: the Red and Blue Chair. Each part of the chair has its own shape, position and color, and the viewer can read it one slat at a time. The Schröder House takes that logic up to full size. In *Architects' Drawings* (2005), Kendra Schank Smith notes that Rietveld sketched his Zigzag chair in three dimensions before building a model. For someone used to thinking in objects, in bars and sheets, a house can be an assembly too.

At the same time, in Berlin, Mies van der Rohe was also dropping the closed room. In his brick country house project of 1924, according to Jean-Louis Cohen (pp. 35-40), the walls touch only at their ends and space flows from one room to the next. Cohen lists De Stijl among Mies's influences. For how he went on from there, see [Neue Nationalgalerie: A Big Steel Roof and a Nearly Empty Room](/en/cong-trinh/neue-nationalgalerie-mies/). The two men differ, but both wanted walls to stop being a box.

## Why Not All Concrete

Rietveld wanted to pour the whole house in concrete, but concrete was too expensive for a small house. In the end it went only into the foundations and the balconies. The walls are plastered brick, the doors and floors are wood, the floors rest on timber beams, and the frame uses steel girders and wire mesh. So the house looks assembled from light flat sheets, while underneath it is ordinary material of its day. The idea came first, and the material had to give way to the budget.

The client didn't let the idea make her life hard, either. The house has a garage, even though Schröder had no car. From the 1960s a motorway ran right in front, and according to ArchEyes Rietveld felt it weakened the link between the house and its surroundings. However beautiful a house is, it lives in a neighborhood that keeps changing.

## Taking It Home

Your home may not need sliding walls, but Mrs. Schröder's question works anywhere: does this wall really have to stand still forever? I find it fits small homes best, where one room has to do many jobs in a day. A panel, a thick curtain on a track, a cabinet on wheels are all distant relatives of the idea. It is also worth remembering that the Schröder House belongs to a client with opinions and an architect willing to listen, so the first lesson may be right there.

Try this one evening: stand in the middle of your living room or bedroom and imagine one wall in your home that slides. Where would it go, what would you see in the morning, and what would you see at night?
