Hold a necklace by both ends and let it sag between your hands. You don't have to draw anything. The curve appears on its own, every time. Now flip it over in your head: the sag becomes an upright arch.

This example is mine, not anyone's memory. But it sits close to a real working method of Antoni Gaudí, who let a string find the shape of an arch for him instead of drawing one and guessing whether it would stand.

## Hang the String, Add the Weights

According to my notes on Kendra Schank Smith's *Architects' Drawings* (2005), Gaudí used a hanging model called a *funicular*, which my notes also call a *polyfunicular*. Small weights hang from the strings. Look at the cover photo: thousands of little bags tied to strings, drooping in several sagging tiers. Smith writes that this model shows the stresses in the arches, and that it was Gaudí's main design tool. The sketch was secondary.

That sentence deserves a slow read. For many architects the drawing is where thinking happens. For Gaudí it happened in the model, and paper came afterward to record it.

His workshop in the basement of the Sagrada Familia, again according to Smith, held plaster models and decorative details.

## Why a Hanging String Becomes an Arch

This part is my own explanation, not the book's. A string can only be pulled. When you hang it between two points and add weights, it settles into a curve where every stretch is pulled and none is bent. Flip that shape over and the pulling turns into squeezing. A stone arch, stone being good at squeezing, that follows exactly that curve has every stone pressed against its neighbors.

So the hanging model had already done the calculation: it gave the right load-bearing curve for the arches. My vault note says the strings give the compressive structure but not the volume. I read that as: the strings give you the bones, and there is no flesh yet.

| | The string model gives | The string model doesn't give |
|---|---|---|
| The curve | Right for the forces | Not a shape to look at |
| Volume | None, only thin threads | How thick the vault is, how big the column |
| Inner space | Not yet visible | What the room looks like |

## Photograph, Flip, Draw On Top

![Three steps: a hanging string with weights, a photo flipped into an arch, then volume drawn over the photo](/images/gaudi-mo-hinh-day-xich-en.svg)

Here is what Smith chose to show. The Gaudí sketch she uses is the interior of the Colonia Güell church, drawn in ink, soft pencil and chalk on an inverted photograph of the string model (p. 121). After the shot, the photo is turned over so the sagging strings stand up as arches. Gaudí laid soft pencil or chalk over them to build volume: vaults, columns, openings.

That meant he didn't have to construct a perspective. The structure was already right in the photo, so he only had to decide on the form, and he could decide fast. Smith compares this to laying tracing paper over a drawing: use what you know to find what you don't. What he knew was the curve of the forces. What he didn't know was how the room inside would feel.

In terms of the six jobs of a sketch that I describe in [Drawing to See, Not to Show Off](/en/nghe-thuat/ve-de-thay/), this is "envisioning" resting on "designing." The photo of the model took care of choosing the form by force; the sketch took care of showing a place that didn't exist yet.

## Form That Comes from Force

Gaudí belongs to the sculptural and organic strand, where the shape of the building is the first concern. According to *The Fundamentals of Architecture* (AVA, 2007), the Sagrada Familia, still unfinished, and Park Güell in Barcelona have thick decoration and look sculpted rather than built. The stone seems loose, light and open, against the prejudice that stone is heavy.

Put those two ideas side by side and something interesting happens. The organic strand is often suspected of shape for the eye's sake. Gaudí's recipe runs the other way: the forces decide the shape. Smith also mentions his view of ornament, that nothing is superfluous and it contains only the material conditions that make it useful. Structure and decoration follow one principle.

In [The Church of the Light: A Dark Room with Just One Slit of Light](/en/cong-trinh/nha-tho-anh-sang-ando/), Ando starts from a sketch he never erases, trimming an image in his head. Gaudí took another road: he let the material, a string and some weights, say in advance what the shape would be. The two don't cancel each other. One asks the hand, the other asks gravity.

## Very Little Is Left

One thing has to be said plainly. Gaudí's models, drawings and personal records were largely destroyed in 1936. So the Colonia Güell sketch is one of only a few that remain, and each architect in Smith's book likewise has just one sketch chosen. My notes warn that you can't read his whole working process from one sample. This piece tells one method only, not the whole man.

The model in the cover photo is described as the string model of the Colonia Güell church, now shown in the museum of the Sagrada Familia. My library notes don't mention this, so I give it only as the photo's caption on Wikimedia Commons says.

## Try It at Home

Hang a necklace or a piece of string between two nails and add a few paper clips as weights. Photograph it with your phone, then flip the image over (every editing tool has a flip button). You now have an arch.

Draw on the photo: a column at each foot, an opening at the top. What kind of house shows up, and how is it different from the ones you draw when you pick up a pen over a blank page?
