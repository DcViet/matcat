This example is mine, not a true story. Say a craftsman takes on three jobs in the same year: a few huts in a coastal pine grove, a roof for a picture gallery in a country of harsh sun, and the cladding for a tower in the middle of a city. What happens if he carries the same set of drawings to all three? The huts will be stuffy, the gallery will glare, and the tower will look like it landed from somewhere else.

Renzo Piano took on jobs very much like these three, and he did not bring one set of drawings along. This piece is about why.

## A builder's son

Piano was born in 1937 in Genoa, into a family of builders. According to Colin Amery, who wrote the text that went with the exhibition when Piano received the Pritzker Prize in 1998, he grew up in an Italy rebuilding after the war, where construction was a way of making ordinary life possible again. He valued the process of building and felt at home on a construction site.

Piano himself says architecture is "both art and service." Amery notes that the prize jury weighed it the same way: art on one side, function and people on the other.

One more detail from this portrait stuck with me. Amery calls Piano original but not revolutionary. Each of his solutions comes from an analysis meant to make the building work as well as it can. He experiments only when the new thing is the smart answer to the problem.

## The Pompidou: services move outside

In 1971 Piano and Richard Rogers won the competition for the Centre Pompidou in Paris. Piano was 35. They dropped the idea of an "inverted pyramid" and chose a clear rectangular block, like a city block. They wanted a cheerful urban machine.

Rogers's signature move was pushing water pipes, ducts, stairs, and lifts out to the edge so the floors inside stay empty. The Lyon catalogue puts Foster, Rogers, and Piano in the same lineage, Team 4 and the Pompidou. In the cover photo you can see white braces with round joints and a white ventilation pipe right beside the old townhouses. I am only describing what is visible in the picture.

What interests me is that Piano did not accept the label "high-tech." He saw it as a gentle jab at an obsession with technology. His foundational partner was the engineer Peter Rice of Ove Arup, and the two worked together until Rice died in 1992. After the Pompidou opened in 1977, Piano was worn out. Amery treats that as a big lesson in teamwork and in reinventing how things get built.

## Four places, four materials

After the Pompidou, if he had simply repeated that white steel frame everywhere, we would call him a designer of one style. But the list of works in Amery's text points the other way.

![A four-card diagram: the Menil in Houston uses light concrete leaves for the Texas sun; Tjibaou in Nouméa uses timber and vents that catch the wind; Padre Pio in Foggia uses a 50-meter stone arch; Potsdamer Platz in Berlin is clad in terracotta](/images/renzo-piano-cach-cong-trinh-duoc-lam-en.svg)

- **Menil Collection, Houston (1986).** The museum sits among low-rise houses, so it is not "neutral" like an ordinary gallery. The roof is made of light concrete "leaves" that filter the Texas sun, so natural light changes over time while the paintings stay safe. The walls are clad in timber to stay cool and to suit the clapboard houses nearby. Piano calls light a building element you cannot touch.
- **Cy Twombly Pavilion (1995).** A square box lit from above, with concrete surfaces, like a modest temple at the foot of the Menil "Parthenon."
- **Tjibaou Cultural Centre, Nouméa (competition 1991, completed 1998).** Ten tall conical timber huts in a pine grove, a contemporary reworking of Kanak architecture. Adjustable vents catch the Pacific wind, and when it blows the building "sings." The book praises it for avoiding "native kitsch," and for the fact that the team came to the island "without baggage," bringing only skills learned from the place.
- **Padre Pio church, Foggia.** Piano returns to stone. The dome is carried by a stone arch spanning 50 meters, said to be the longest stone span built as of 1998. He keeps asking what can be done with stone now.
- **Potsdamer Platz, Berlin (from 1996).** Around 600,000 square meters of land for 40,000 people to live and work. The Daimler-Benz tower is clad in terracotta, a material Piano was reviving and used to tie the whole district together.

Gathering these into one shared question is my own reading; the book does not put it that way. But set side by side, the idea is fairly clear: concrete in Houston, timber in Nouméa, stone in Foggia, terracotta in Berlin. The material changes with the place, and the question stays the same.

## The workshop and the people

A question like that needs somewhere to be tested. The Punta Nave workshop in Genoa, built with UNESCO on a cliff above the sea, is both workshop and research lab, with a glass roof and louvers. The book says people there study plant fibers from Senegal and grow bamboo and agave on the spot.

Piano also had another starting point. According to Kendra Schank Smith in *Architects' Drawings* (2005), he began working with Jean Prouvé in the mid-1960s, and Smith reads that as a sign of fabrication shaping how he thinks about form. The Lyon catalogue adds that he worked for Louis Kahn in Philadelphia. If you read [Kimbell: Making the Sun Take a Detour Before It Touches the Paintings](/en/cong-trinh/kimbell-kahn/), you will see a small link: Kahn had a design for the Menil museum in Houston that was never built, and the Menil that stands there is Piano's.

The Lyon catalogue also suggests a way to split three contrasting names: Ando is minimalist, Gehry is sculptural, and Piano is craft. [The Church of the Light: A Dark Room with Just One Slit of Light](/en/cong-trinh/nha-tho-anh-sang-ando/) is about the first of those three.

## Reading with care

Two cautions. My main source is Amery's text, written for the prize's own exhibition, so its tone leans toward praise. Second, I am working only from books and a catalogue, so I cannot say how these buildings look today.

If I had to say what makes Piano Piano, I think it is not any one material or joint, but the habit of asking before drawing.

Try a small experiment. Pick an object in your home, a chair, a door, a step, and ask it: who made you, how, and why from this material and not another? The answer might change how you see the whole room.
