Think of a kitchen and a living room in your own house. The kitchen has pots, a chimney, wires, a gas tank, and everything in it has to be measured and calculated. The living room is different: you sit and talk, and nothing there can be measured. The two places ask for different ways of thinking, yet every house has to put them side by side.

This example is mine, not a memory. It is much smaller, but it sits close to the problem Louis Kahn faced at the Salk Institute, a biological research institute on a bluff above the Pacific in La Jolla, California, built between about 1959 and 1965.

## A Scientist Who Wanted to Invite Picasso

In *Essential Texts* (edited by Twombly, 2003), Kahn tells of the day Jonas Salk visited the Richards building in Philadelphia, one of his earlier works. Salk asked how big it was. Kahn gave a figure, and Salk said that was what he needed. Then Salk added a remark Kahn never forgot: he wanted to invite Picasso to the institute. The area Kahn quoted changes from telling to telling, about 91,000, 100,000 or 109,000 square feet, so I will only say it was around a hundred thousand.

For Kahn that remark changed the whole problem. As he understood it, medical research concerns people in general, and biological technique alone would not be enough, so there had to be room for art. From there he divided the institute into three parts.

1. The laboratories: the part that is built, suited to clean air and floor area that can be adjusted.
2. The studies: teak, looking onto the courtyard and the sea, set on arcaded terraces, separated from the laboratories and not looking straight at them.
3. The meeting house: Kahn described it as a place for gathering, with a dining room split into small seminar rooms, a library, a gym, around an unnamed hall about 60 by 60 feet (in one place he says 70 by 70).

He called these, in his own way, two worlds: the measurable and the unmeasurable.

## The Laboratories: An Architecture of Things That Must Serve

For the measurable part Kahn was very strict. The idea was already there at Richards: tell the served rooms from the servant places, such as storage, stairs, exhaust shafts, and service runs. At Richards he pulled the tower of work rooms away from the brick tower holding the stairs and exhaust, following a rule that sounds like a moral: the air you breathe must not touch the air you throw away.

At Salk he carried the idea into the vertical dimension. According to the MoMA educators guide (2007), each block has six floors, three laboratory floors alternating with three service floors. Each service floor is about 9 feet high, a little over 2.7 meters. Together with the engineer August Komendant, Kahn used Vierendeel beams spanning about 60 feet.

One detail stays with me. His first scheme made the people servicing the pipes crawl through a cramped space. He dropped it and traded it for a service floor as tall as the laboratory itself. It cost more, but to him it was truer to what the place is: a "room for machines." According to the student material on Salk, the laboratories have no partitions and the lights slide on rails.

A word of caution is in order. In *The Architecture of the Well-tempered Environment* (1969), Banham warns that after Richards, "service towers" easily turn into decoration if they do not really contain services. And Scully, in *Arquitectura Viva* (2001), points out that the Richards towers lack sun shading, so users stuck foil on the glass themselves. A tidy form is not always tidy in use.

## The Empty Courtyard and Barragán's Advice

Between the two rows of laboratories Kahn first imagined a garden. He sketched many versions, including a row of poplars. According to the article by Antonio Amado Lorenzo in *Revista EGA*, in early 1965 he phoned and then wrote to Luis Barragán asking for advice. Barragán came to La Jolla in early 1966. He touched the concrete wall, then said not to put a single tree or blade of grass there. It should be a stone plaza, and it would have "one more facade, facing the sky."

![A conceptual diagram: two rows of laboratories alternating with service floors, a stone plaza between them with a channel of water running toward the sea and an arrow pointing up to the sky](/images/salk-kahn-san-huong-len-troi-en.svg)

The plaza was finally paved in travertine. Kahn wanted slate, but slate cost more in California. A narrow channel of water runs down the middle of the plaza, leads the eye out to the Pacific, and drops to a lower court. The irrigation pipes prepared for the trees had to take on a new meaning, as drainage. Who came up with the water channel is still disputed; Twombly records this.

Kahn said he had been "tied up" by the idea of a garden for years, and Barragán was the one who untied him. In his Boston talk of 1966 (*Essential Texts*, p. 197) he mentions Barragán's plaza. According to the same Lorenzo article, a water channel of more than 60 meters running toward the sea was already in Kahn's sketches, and Barragán trimmed it down to a single line. The author argues that this scheme was "more Kahn" than Kahn himself had imagined, and that Kahn accepted it immediately. Barragán was paid a consultant's fee like any other expert. Kahn also once remarked that Barragán's garden had only one small tap and yet felt "immense."

Here is how I read it, and this is my own idea, not the books': the empty courtyard is a shared resting place for the two worlds. On one side are pipes, steel, measurement. On the other are teak, desks, the sea. In between there is only stone, water and sky, so neither side crowds the other.

## Two Ways of Using Emptiness

The concrete at Salk is not clad or painted. According to the Boston talk, Kahn designed the formwork himself, kept the pour joints and tie holes, plugged the tie holes with lead, and the concrete here is slightly pink. The MoMA guide records that he called concrete "liquid stone." The concrete blocks stand beside a stone plaza without feeling foreign to it.

Set Salk next to Ando's [The Church of the Light: A Dark Room with Just One Slit of Light](/en/cong-trinh/nha-tho-anh-sang-ando/) and you see that both lean on emptiness, but each uses it in a different way:

| | Salk (Kahn) | Church of the Light (Ando) |
|---|---|---|
| The emptiness is | A stone plaza between two rows of buildings | A cross-shaped slit of light in the wall |
| It faces | The sea and the sky | The light itself |
| Its job | A resting place between two worlds | The one event of the room |

I arranged this table myself; it does not come from the books.

## Bringing It Home

If you bring Salk home to a Vietnamese house, the hardest part is Barragán's. He said to take out every tree, but in a sunny climate a stone plaza with no shade easily turns glaring and hot. To do it you need water, the shadow the buildings cast, and light-colored materials to keep the plaza cool. In our climate there is also heavy rain and humidity, so it needs rethinking. Kahn met the sun-shading problem in Luanda, as I told in the post [Good Light Is Not Plenty of Light](/en/bai-viet/anh-sang-tot-khong-phai-anh-sang-nhieu/).

What can be brought home is smaller. One is to separate the places that serve from the places that are served, like kitchen and living room, plumbing and bedroom. Two is to leave an empty space between two parts that think differently, and not rush to fill it. A small light well will do. As for a patch of sky for a whole room, I told that in [The Pantheon: A Whole Room with One Round Hole Looking Up at the Sky](/en/cong-trinh/pantheon-anh-sang-tu-tren/).

Try it once. Take a sheet of paper with your house plan on it and color the places that "must be calculated" (kitchen, bathrooms, stairs, pipes) one color, and the places where you live another. Then see whether there is any empty space between the two colors.

If there isn't, where would you leave room to look up at the sky?
